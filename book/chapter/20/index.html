Below the old kiln, where the town’s drainage meets the river meadows, sat a narrow stone canal lock that nobody liked to hurry through. Two waters pressed against its timber doors: sweetened river brine on the townward side, and tart, bitter orchard runoff coming down off the plum terrace.

The lock’s oak gates had swollen tight in the rain. Worse, they answered to two separate charters: the upper leaf belonged to Boon.land’s commons and opened for nothing, while the lower leaf had been bolted with brass rail-pins by an old toll guild that still expected every gallon to declare its errand.

Try to crank both sluices at once and the currents fought each other into a standstill froth. Along the muddy bank, ink-herons stood so motionless watching the stalemate that their reflections had dried matte black onto the wet clay.

In the center of the lock chamber, seam-otters rolled happily along the unblended boundary, one paw in sweet water and one in bitter, knotting green rush reeds into small floating rings. Directly above them, a serious mime perched on the empty air six inches over the swirl and paddled smoothly across with an invisible oar, paying neither water a toll.

The lockkeeper leaned both elbows on his iron crank and sighed. “Two springs, one bed, the bridge folk say — easy to say when you don’t have swollen oak jammed in both grooves.”

Standing dry on the narrow stone sill between the gates, the figure lifted one straight ink arm on the tower’s first chime, waited through the second, and pointed cleanly at the upper rail-pin on the third. The lockkeeper shrugged, pulled one pin alone, and let the sweet water drop cleanly into the chamber. Three chimes later he pulled the lower pin instead. Neither gate had to agree with the other; alternating every third chime, both currents passed straight down the reach.

On the dry coping wall beside the lower sluice sat Mr. BaneWAP — smaller, tidier, and far quieter than he had been back in Book One, before his schemes gave out at the confluence. Beside his crust rested a little stone jar of dark orchard preserve, sharp with bitter quince rind.

He dipped a tin spoon in, tasted thoughtfully, and offered Boof the clean back of the handle to sniff. Not a whiff of rented wind or tax ledgers clung to him; only cooked fruit and a single honest pinch of baneberry keeping the sweetness from turning cloying.

“A pinch sets a jam,” Mr. BaneWAP remarked mildly, watching the seam-otters ride out through the lower gate. “A whole bowl ruins supper. I admit it took me twelve chapters to tell the difference.”

Boof gave BaneWAP one polite tail-thump against the coping stone — neither forgiveness nor suspicion, simply the respect a working dog shows an instrument that has finally found its right measure.

Below the lock, the purple-dark and pale-bitter streams ran side by side into the dusk, seam-otters rolling between them like stitches in a coat. On the sill, the figure stepped down onto the towpath, dry to the ankles, with his hat sailing smoothly above the mist.

At the tablePlay in this chapter

A stone canal lock chamber between the old kiln and the river meadows, where berry-sweet brine and bitter orchard runoff press against swollen oak gates pinned with brass rail-pins.

Light Low winter amber shining across two unblended water colours, with matte-black heron reflections dried onto the clay banks.

People

  • the canal lockkeeper wants both barges through before frost freezes the sluice paddles shut — broad-shouldered, counts chimes under his breath
  • Mr. BaneWAP (re-sweetened) wants to perfect the exact pinch of bitterness that wakes a berry preserve without spoiling it — still theatrical, now oddly courteous, carries a pocket tin spoon
  • a toll-guild boatman wants a receipt that proves his barge passed through without paying twice — grumbles at otters, secretly feeds them crusts

Hooks

  1. A reed ring knotted by seam-otters has snagged the lower rail-pin; retrieve it without disturbing the ink-herons' dried bank reflections.
  2. Two barges — one carrying sweet flour, one carrying bitter cider apples — arrive at opposite gates at dusk and both claim the third chime.
  3. Mr. BaneWAP asks the party to carry a small stone jar of bitter jam to the bakehouse vault before the feast, swearing there is no scheme inside it.

Roll a d6

  1. The lock chimes a third beat and the oak gate swings lightly on its own, leaving a rush-knot floating at the bow.
  2. A mime paddles past mid-air across the flooded lock, throwing up no spray at all.
  3. Two seam-otters trade a brass rail-pin back and forth across the boundary line until someone offers an apple core.
  4. An ink-heron lifts off the mudbank, leaving its shadow printed behind like a woodblock stamp.
  5. Swollen timber groans; forcing both gates together jams the crank until somebody counts to three.
  6. Mr. BaneWAP offers a tiny taste from his tin spoon — sharp enough to clear a head clouded by too many speeches.

Worth carrying out

  • Seam-otters — Follow a knotted rush ring to find the exact boundary where two quarrelling rules can run side by side without collapsing.
  • Rail-pins — Pull one pin at a time on a steady three-count to open stubborn gates that jam under hurry.
