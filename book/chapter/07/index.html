Chapter Seven: Blueberry Oath

In which an oath is paid in fruit.

Every wish is paid twice: once by the wisher, once by someone who never hears about it.

3 min read

Boof

small want

1 min

The great market stone stood at the center of the square, its granite top worn smooth and dished by centuries of pressing palms. Boof had come to do the one thing the council could not do for her and BaneWAP could not undo: bind herself, in public, to the wish. For an oath here was understood exactly as the Genie had taught her to understand keys — it was a key given away in daylight, handed to the whole watching town at once, so that no one hand could ever secretly grow it. The public half was the words. The private half was paid in fruit, pressed into the rock where centuries of sworn juice had gone before. And the witness was the egg itself, set warm upon the lip of the stone: an unspent wish, watching a promise being made about its own spending.

Boof pressed a blueberry into the stone. Skin split; juice slipped into the groove worn by other paws and palms. She would spend the wish when the want was true. Not for spite, not for show. Not because a crowd was waiting. The square fell quiet enough to hear a stall-cloth flap. Beneath her other paw, the egg grew warmer.

Shadow
1 min

But every oath sworn in noon light casts one. The shadow lay across the grooves, dog-shaped, egg-shaped, cold, and it was not BaneWAP's — it was the oath's own dark half, the clause nobody reads aloud. The Genie read it anyway, because pricing things was the one habit he had not liquidated.

"Understand what you have promised to spend," he said quietly. "A wish redirects momentum. Redirected momentum lands somewhere. Every spent wish — mine, yours, anyone's — owes one coincidence to a stranger. A cart delayed, a letter misdelivered, a rain moved to a different field. Someone always pays the correction, and they are never at the ceremony."

After the crowd thinned, Boof stayed, chin against the granite. Purple juice dried beside older stains. Somewhere, a stranger would be late because she had wished: a cart missed, a letter lost, rain on the wrong field.

She could neither find them nor warn them. The oath gave her no way around that. She sat until the stone cooled beneath her chin. Around her, the square returned to its smaller business. A merchant shook rain from an awning, a child chased a rolling spool, and two neighbors resumed an argument they had set aside for the ceremony. None of them knew which stranger would catch the turn of her wish.

Perhaps that stranger was having an ordinary afternoon too, expecting a letter, counting the hours to supper. Boof watched the spool lodge between two stones. The child knelt to free it. She stayed a little longer, until the crowd had become people again.

She pressed one last blueberry into the central groove until the purple juice welled up and filled the old gold-veined cracks. Payment that smelled true. The lattice-edged shadow at the alley mouth withdrew without a sound — an oath witnessed in public is a poor thing to bid on. Boof lifted the egg back into her sling and turned her nose south, toward the ancient well that keeps every face without stealing a single soul.
