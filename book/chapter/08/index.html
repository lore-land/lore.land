Chapter Eight: Songs of the Watering Eye

In which water keeps a face without stealing it.

The well keeps every face; the tavern keeps every round. Between them, all debts are known.

3 min read

Boof

berry memory

1 min

The Watering Eye was a well before it was a witness, and a witness long before anyone built a tavern leaning over it like a drinker over a confession. Boof came in under the sagging floorboards and set her front paws on the mossy coping. The water below was black and perfectly still, and it did not show her own face first.

It showed her an older one — a woman mid-wish, mouth open on a want she had never said aloud — and under that face, folded like pressed linen going down into the dark, every other face the well had ever held. From the barrel-head behind her came the Genie's quoting voice, brisk as a market morning: "I can make you famous by Tuesday, or respected by November. Fame is cheap — reality already leans that way for loud people. Respect requires corrections. Choose."

A face said into deep water is not stolen; it is folded, and it stays the property of whoever wore it. The well kept the faces the way good ground keeps rain: patiently, and without letting anyone else spend them.

Fold
1 min

All night the Genie dealt in wants. Name one, hear the price, pay or fold. An empire? Quite manageable; the world was forever tipping toward a throne. Supper everyone agreed on? He counted the company twice and quoted three coincidences and a fortnight of Thursdays. At the bar, the regular raised his glass. “Six violations of the natural order,” the Genie told him. “Lager,” said the regular. The Genie wrote it down. After a moment, he underlined it.

A line of ants had left a crumb on the barrel-head at dusk. He quoted them nothing, struck a match for their torch-bearer, and wrote nothing down. Old customers.

Two beavers took the whole evening over a dam. They wanted the wording exact: the bend, the height, the arrangement of the sticks. By the time they had it right they had drawn it on the table in spilled lager, argued the drawing into a better one, and gone out to build it before the Genie could quote a price. He watched them go with something like professional respect. Beavers, he told Boof, always wished slowly enough to finish the job themselves.

The Unpriceable Want

Near closing, he tried Boof again. “Anything. Name it.” She thought of the furrow, the warmth, a trail with something real at its end. His needle swung beyond the numbers and came back blank. He reset it. She tried again. Same small click, same empty dial. Boof lowered the egg onto the wet stone around the well. Heat spread under her paws, through the coping, down.

The folded faces stirred. One by one they turned toward the warmth the way the berries once had. Then the water said, in the plain way water says things, what every face down there had in common: each one owed a coincidence to a stranger. The egg — an unspent wish — was the debt still standing warm above the fold. The well could not price it either.

It only named it, and kept the account open. Boof leaned over the rim without touching the water. Some faces were clear enough to count the lines around a mouth; others drifted beneath reflections of the tavern windows. A laugh came through the floorboards overhead, and for a moment she could not tell whether one of the faces had laughed too.

She moved the egg closer. Ripples reached the far wall and came back through the reflected light. The Genie stood beside her with his instruments shut. She was glad he had stopped asking her to name it.

"What did I win?" Boof wondered, taking the egg back into her sling. The Genie had priced fame, thrones, and six lagers, and had folded on her — the only player at the table whose want his game could not hold. Behind her the regular raised one finger at the dark, on principle.

She climbed toward the roofs with the well's answer warming her ribs. The egg was a debt. The faces below had turned toward it together, though no voice had called them. Above the ridge, eight old notes hung in the dark. She listened for the missing one and felt it humming against her chest.
