Chapter Eight: Songs of the Watering Eye

In which water keeps a face without stealing it.

The well keeps every face; the tavern keeps every round. Between them, all debts are known.

The Watering Eye was a well before it was a witness, and a witness long before anyone built a tavern leaning over it like a drinker over a confession. Boof came in under the sagging floorboards, nose first through spilled lager and wet rope, and set her front paws on the mossy coping. The water below was black and perfectly still, and it did not show her own face first.

It showed her an older one — a woman mid-wish, mouth open on a want she had never said aloud — and under that face, folded like pressed linen going down into the dark, every other face the well had ever held. From the barrel-head behind her came the Genie's quoting voice, brisk as a market morning: "I can make you famous by Tuesday, or respected by November. Fame is cheap — reality already leans that way for loud people. Respect requires corrections. Choose."

A face said into deep water is not stolen; it is folded, and it stays the property of whoever wore it. The well kept the faces the way good ground keeps rain: patiently, and without letting anyone else spend them.

Something down there smelled of a tin cup left in a stream. Then Boof saw them: chalk-pale fish no longer than a spoon, swimming between the faces. Remembrancers, the tavern called them. They went through the faces the way a laundress goes through a basket, tucking a loose chin under, smoothing a brow flat, folding each face gently so it would fit beside the others. One rose to look at Boof and turned politely away, as if she were a face it had not been asked to keep.

Overhead the well-winch creaked. The tavern bucket came down past her nose on its wet hemp rope, sank, and was wound up again to the winch song, honey-slow, four beats a turn, so it would not knock anyone on the way. It was a still night. On still nights the bucket comes up holding a face instead of water. The potboy looked in, said "Evening," to whoever it was, and let it back down. The kitchen drew from the rain-butt instead, and the soup tasted faintly of roof.

All night the Genie dealt in wants. Name one, hear the price, pay or fold. An empire? Quite manageable; the world was forever tipping toward a throne. Supper everyone agreed on? His needle swung so wide it rang against its case; he counted the company twice and quoted three coincidences and a fortnight of Thursdays. At the bar, the regular raised his glass. “Six violations of the natural order,” the Genie told him. “Lager,” said the regular. The Genie wrote it down. After a moment, he underlined it.

The locals queued with their wants the way they queued at the winch: a roof mended before the rains, a cousin forgiven, a goat talked back over a wall. They held pewter tankards while they waited and drank them while they thought. A crock of pickled walnuts went round. Every want was smaller than a throne, and every one cost more.

A line of torch-ants had come up through a knot-hole at dusk, a long walk from the field, and left a crumb on the barrel-head; the well-damp had put their torch out on the stair. He quoted them nothing, struck a match for their torch-bearer, and wrote nothing down. Old customers.

Two beavers, smelling of willow bark and creek mud, took the whole evening over a dam. They wanted the wording exact: the bend, the height, the arrangement of the sticks. By the time they had it right they had drawn it on the table in spilled lager, argued the drawing into a better one, and gone out to build it before the Genie could quote a price. He watched them go with something like professional respect. Beavers, he told Boof, always wished slowly enough to finish the job themselves.

Boof had smelled their work all the way up the creek: the water held back in steps, pond over pond, so that it came down to the well slow and clear and never all at once. That was why the Watering Eye had never run dry, and why its faces lay so still. The beavers had never asked the tavern for anything. The tavern left the bark off its firewood on the back step for them, and nobody wrote that down either. Slow work, Boof thought, like the ants: nobody in a hurry, and the whole valley drinking from it.

Later the taproom sang: a reel about a miller's daughter, too quick, with clapping, and a carter from over the pass clapped quickest of all. Down the shaft the bucket began to swing. The regular put one hand flat on the bar. Table by table the clapping stopped, the carter's last, and Boof felt it through the boards before anyone heard it: four slow beats a turn, under the reel like a floor under dancers. “The winch song underneath,” said the potboy, winding. “They all are.” The bucket came up even, and knocked nobody.

Near closing, he tried Boof again. “Anything. Name it.” She thought of the furrow, the warmth, a trail with something real at its end. His want-needle swung past the numbers and came back blank. He reset it with a thumbnail. She tried again. Same small click, same empty dial. He had the baize case half open to mend the needle when he remembered the clerks at the Rift: listen before you mend. He held it to his ear instead. It ticked as sweetly as the day he bought it.

He tried the resolution glass, which cannot see a want smaller than a loaf. It found one at once, in the potboy, who had been eyeing the heel of the last loaf all night. Of Boof it showed only wet stones and an egg. The Genie folded the glass shut with the face of a man whose tools have all, politely, declined.

Boof lowered the egg onto the wet stone around the well. Heat spread under her paws, through the coping, down. The folded faces stirred. One by one they turned toward the warmth the way the berries once had.

The remembrancers went quick among them, re-folding every face that turned, and for once they fell behind. One rose to the egg's reflection and nosed along it for an edge to fold. It found none. It hung there a moment, pale as a fingernail, then sank back to its work.

Then the water said, in the plain way water says things, what every face down there had in common: each one owed a coincidence to a stranger. The egg — an unspent wish — was the debt still standing warm above the fold. The well could not price it either.

It only named it, and kept the account open. Boof leaned over the rim without touching the water. Some faces were clear enough to count the lines around a mouth; others drifted beneath reflections of the tavern windows. A laugh came through the floorboards overhead, and for a moment she could not tell whether one of the faces had laughed too.

She moved the egg closer. Ripples reached the far wall and came back through the reflected light. The Genie stood beside her with his instruments shut in their baize case. She was glad he had stopped asking her to name it.

"What did I win?" Boof wondered, taking the egg back into her sling. The Genie had priced fame, thrones, and six lagers, and had folded on her — the only player at the table whose want his game could not hold. Behind her the regular raised one finger at the dark, on principle.

She climbed toward the roofs with the well's answer warming her ribs. Behind her, slow, four beats a turn, the potboy was singing up the last bucket of the night. The egg was a debt. The faces below had turned toward it together, though no voice had called them. Above the ridge, eight old notes hung in the dark. She listened for the missing one and felt it humming against her chest.

At the tablePlay in this chapter

The Watering Eye: a tavern built leaning over an ancient well like a drinker over a confession. Down under the sagging floorboards, mossy coping rings black water that shows not your face but older ones, folded like pressed linen into the dark, with chalk-pale fish moving among them. Up in the taproom: pewter tankards, a crock of pickled walnuts going round, a long table where an out-of-season genie prices wants, and a well-winch whose slow four-beat song is under every tune anyone sings.

Light Amber lamplight leaking down through gaps in the floor onto still water that reflects earlier lamps than tonight's. Toward closing the lamps go out one by one, until the folded faces in the well are the brightest thing in the building.

People

  • the potboy wants the heel of the last loaf, and the last bucket wound up without knocking anybody — Says “Evening” to whatever the bucket brings up; sings four beats a turn without noticing he is doing it.
  • the regular wants another lager, and nobody clapping over the winch — One raised finger, one word, and — when the room forgets the song underneath — one hand laid flat on the bar.
  • the Genie wants a want he can price properly; thrones bore him, suppers fascinate him — Brisk as a market morning; qualifies everything, counts the company twice, writes it down and, after a moment, underlines it.
  • the elder beaver wants the exact wording of a dam: the bend, the height, the arrangement of the sticks — Smells of willow bark and creek mud; argues the younger one's drawings into better ones in spilled lager, slowly, and leaves to build before anyone can quote a price.

Hooks

  1. A face that won't fold. On a still night the bucket came up with a face the remembrancers cannot fold flat; it keeps turning toward the stair. A face said into deep water stays its owner's — it can't be kept or spent — and somebody in the taproom tonight wore this one once and has forgotten the want they said into the well. Find them, and let them decide whether to remember.
  2. The Genie's game. Name a want, hear the price, pay or fold. Thrones are cheap; a supper everyone agrees on costs three coincidences and a fortnight of Thursdays. Each player names something they want, the table decides what they will pay and what they will fold, and somebody out in the valley catches the correction.
  3. The rope is going. The well-winch's hemp is fraying and the cooper wants to splice it tonight, before market. The potboy says listen before you mend: the winch sings a beat flat a turn before it fails, and a splice in the wrong place will knock a face on the way up. Someone has to listen through a whole night of songs to find the flat beat.

Roll a d6

  1. A line of torch-ants comes up through a knot-hole with a crumb for the barrel-head; their torch went out on the damp stair. Somebody has to strike a match. The Genie never charges them.
  2. A reel starts, too quick, with clapping, and down the shaft the bucket begins to swing. Somebody has to get the room to stop clapping and find the winch song underneath.
  3. The bucket comes up holding a face instead of water, and the face looks at one of the party as if it knows them. The potboy says “Evening” and waits to see whether they will let it back down.
  4. A local in the Genie's queue wants a sulking stove taught to draw. The Genie quotes three coincidences. Anyone with a chimney-brush could fix it by supper, for a bowl of soup that tastes faintly of roof.
  5. A remembrancer rises through the faces, looks one of the party over, and turns politely away, as if they were a face it had not been asked to keep. Yet.
  6. The Genie's resolution glass, swinging idly across the room, finds a want larger than a loaf. It is not the one anybody said out loud.

Worth carrying out

  • The well-winch — Wound to the four-beat song on a still night, the bucket may come up holding a face instead of water: someone who once said a want into the well. The catch: the face stays its owner's. You may look, greet it and let it back down, never keep it — and the kitchen drinks rain-water till morning.
  • The want-needle — Held near someone, the brass needle circles toward their want, slows, and tries again. The catch: it finds big wants fast and small ones never, and a blank dial does not mean nobody wants anything — only that the want is too small and too true for brass.

A bracework move

{reel & winch}

Does A room where the quick song and the slow one run together without either giving way: for a minute everything inside moves at four beats a turn under whatever is being sung. Thrown tankards arrive gently, a fist lands as a handshake, the bucket comes up without knocking.

Costs Warmth borrowed from the hearth, which drops a notch and sulks; and a debt of tempo — whoever wrote it cannot hurry at anything until the next still night.
