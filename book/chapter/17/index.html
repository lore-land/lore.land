At the windy edge of Council Hill stood the inquiry pavilion: sixteen open pillars over a checkered floor where the iron weathercocks creaked overhead and cross-breeze dust drifted across blotter paper. Inside, three municipal clerks worked briskly, stamping settled answers onto clean slips and shelving them before noon.

It saved a great deal of municipal time, the senior clerk explained to nobody in particular, to file the answers first. When a citizen eventually brought a question up the hill, it could be matched at once to the nearest answer already dry on the shelf.

The trouble with filing answers early is that they come in only two shapes: yes and no. Along the back cedar wall, four hundred pigeonholes sat packed with slips trimmed to fit one half of a hinge. Only one compartment near the top stayed empty, because a round grey shelf-owl sat squarely inside it with one amber eye open and clacked her beak whenever a clerk reached up with a stamp.

The figure placed a square sheet of coarse paper on the blotter. Drawn on it in clean black ink were four right-angled corners pointing out toward four separate edges, with nothing written inside the middle.

The first clerk turned the sheet ninety degrees to see if it became a yes. The second turned it back to see if it became a no. The third slid a ruler along its sides, frowned, and checked the revised form from the parcel gate, which now included a box for what you do not know. Unfortunately the pavilion had not yet received the revised stationery.

Unable to decide which tray held four directions at once, the three clerks copied the drawing onto four separate slips and filed one in Yes, one in No, one in Deferred, and one under Weather. None of the four trays closed flat afterward.

While the clerks debated hinges, Boof worked her nose along the lowest shelf, where forgotten slips gather under blotter shavings. Halfway back, wedged behind a brass paperweight, she caught the unmistakable sweet-slate smell of the market stone and crushed summer blueberry.

She hooked the slip out with one careful claw. It was the unspent coincidence left over from the market oath back in Book One — owed to a stranger, payable on arrival, and signed by nobody because the witness had been warm and had no pen. Two grey oath-moths drifted down from the pavilion rafters, inspected the dried berry groove along its margin, and folded their wings flat on top of it.

The senior clerk peered over the counter. “That,” he said stiffly, “is an orphan liability. We have tried filing it under Asset and under Mischief. Both trays spit it back out.”

The figure reached across the counter — careful not to touch the oath-moths — and tapped the empty pigeonhole where the shelf-owl sat. The owl blinked once, tucked one wing over the blue-stained slip, and settled round as a loaf.

Outside, the weathercocks groaned and swung half a turn toward the town roofs. Boof sneezed a puff of blotter dust off her muzzle and trotted down the hill path with the figure stepping cleanly behind her. In the pavilion, three trays stood ajar, and one empty slot stayed exactly full enough to hold what had not been asked yet.

An open-sided checkered stone pavilion near the crest of Council Hill. Wind off the weathercocks lifts blotter dust across cedar filing walls packed with pre-stamped replies.

Light Cool crisp hill daylight sliced into stripes by sixteen timber pillars, with one amber lamp-oil eye glowing in the highest empty pigeonhole.

People

  • the senior inquiry clerk wants every petition sorted cleanly into Yes or No before the bell — clipped, polite, deeply suspicious of right angles
  • the junior blotter keeper wants to smuggle a box for what you do not know onto the official stationery — ink-stained fingers, whispers sideways while stamping
  • a petitioner from the mill wants an answer to a question nobody has ever written a form for — patient, leaning on a flour stick

Hooks

  1. The pavilion filed an answer three weeks early and now an entire street is trying to invent the question that matches it before the fine comes due.
  2. An unspent coincidence slipped out of the orphan tray in a crosswind; follow the oath-moths across Council Hill before a merchant tries to cash it twice.
  3. The shelf-owl has left her pigeonhole empty for the first time in ten years; discover which unasked question finally walked up the steps.

Roll a d6

  1. A gust lifts twelve pre-stamped YES slips across the checkered tiles; stepping on two at once counts as a municipal quorum.
  2. Two oath-moths alight on a party member's sleeve, weighing whether the last promise spoken aloud was true.
  3. A clerk slides a binary questionnaire across the desk where both boxes read YES IN PART.
  4. Overhead, seven iron weathercocks spin into seven different opinions and stir up a dust devil inside the pillars.
  5. The shelf-owl clacks her beak once; a hidden drawer pops open containing a slip written in blueberry ink.
  6. The plain-line figure folds a blank questionnaire into four corners and sets it upright on the windward sill, where it refuses to blow away.

Worth carrying out

  • Shelf-owls — Set an empty shelf-gap aside in your plans; whatever you refuse to fill early stays open for the right arrival.
  • Oath-moths — Hold a doubtful slip still at noon; if the grey moths settle flat along its crease, the promise underneath holds.
