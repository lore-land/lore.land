The night school opened only after the street lamps were lit and the day’s haul was put away. Every seat along the oak bench held a grown working citizen: Clem Furrow still smelling of harness grease, Ida Bell with laundry starch on her sleeves, the Kettle twins straight off the coal dock, and Noor from the upper mill shaking oat husks from her scarf.

At the far end of the bench sat the quiet new pupil drawn in plain black ink, his bowler hovering smoothly in the tallow warmth. The mistress set one round tin pie, two wooden geometry dividers dusted with rosin, and a spool of waxed twine in the center of the oak table.

“Tonight is rehearsal,” the mistress said, tapping the dark slate board behind her. “Before the Great Table goes out in the plaza, every hand in this room is going to measure a circle honestly and find out why it will not sit still.”

Noor wrapped the twine snug round the pie tin’s rim, pinched the overlap with her thumb, and laid the length straight across the tin’s middle. One width. Two widths. Three widths — and then a brisk, unapologetic sliver left dangling past the edge.

Clem Furrow tried with brass pie-dividers instead, stepping carefully round the paper circle on the bench. Every time he reached the starting dot, the final brass point overshot by the thickness of a knife blade. When one of the Kettle twins pressed the wooden ruler harder against the oak to force the chalk circle shut before dawn, tiny load-beetles inside the old bench grain began to click in dry protest.

“Ease off, Kettle,” Ida Bell laughed softly. “Chalk is cheaper than oak, and you cannot bully a circumference into a whole number.”

Hands that have hauled sacks and wrung wet sheets all day shake slightly when asked to draw fine lines by tallow light. Seeing the dividers tremble on the slate, the mistress did not correct anyone’s grip. Instead she hummed a single low, open note — plain A, steady as a winter hearth — and waited.

Ida joined a fourth above her; Noor took the fifth; Clem and the Kettle twins fell in an octave down, breathing together without counting bars. Inside the steady sound, the trembling stopped. Every wooden ruler and brass point glided calmly to the exact pin at the center of the circle, while three remainder-moths lifted off the chalk ledge and traced wide, unclosing spirals around the lamp.

The figure did not hum — plain lines have no throat — yet he set his shorter right arm cleanly beside Ida’s elbow, steadying the twine while she chalked the neatest arc of the evening.

At the doorframe, Boof rested her chin on her front paws, listening to the low humming fade into tallow crackle. She had watched councils argue over straight lines for weeks; watching six grown neighbours steady one shared circle without forcing its gap shut smelled considerably better.

Before snuffing the lamps, the mistress checked the attendance roll: Clem Furrow, Ida Bell, the Kettle twins, Noor of the mill, and one quiet blank line still kept honest at the bottom. Nobody filled it in. Out in the lane, dawn was still hours away, and the chalk circle on the slate kept its small open sliver intact.

People

  • the night school's mistress wants every grown pupil to leave with steadier hands and an unrounded measure — calm alto, begins a humming fourth whenever elbows tighten
  • Ida Bell wants to draw one circle cleanly after twelve hours at the wash-house — warm laugh, starch-smelling sleeves, fearless with chalk
  • Noor of the mill wants to know why three widths of string never quite cover a millstone's rim — practical, measures twice with her thumbnail
  • Clem Furrow wants the bench oak kept quiet so the load-beetles stop scolding his elbows — deep bass hum, smells of harness wax

Hooks

  1. The school's brass pie-dividers have lost their center pin before the feast rehearsal; borrow a true pin from Bone.land without promising to round the circle off.
  2. A municipal surveyor insists the night school round its circles to an even three so the feast benches fit the plaza; demonstrate why the sliver keeps the tables standing.
  3. Remainder-moths from the chalk ledge have settled on the attendance roll, glowing faintly beside the blank bottom line.

Roll a d6

  1. Load-beetles in the oak bench click sharply whenever someone forces a measurement that does not fit.
  2. Three remainder-moths lift off the sill and trace a glowing spiral that misses its starting point by an inch.
  3. A spool of waxed twine rolls under the bench and comes back knotted cleanly by someone nobody saw bend down.
  4. When the room hums an open fourth together, every lantern flame stops flickering for thirty seconds.
  5. A fresh chalk circle on the slate board refuses to close by a hair's breadth, yet everything drawn inside it stays level.

Worth carrying out

  • Pie-dividers — Step them round any disputed share; they keep the honest remainder visible instead of hiding it.
  • Load-beetles — Listen to the furniture before signing an agreement; a sharp click in the oak warns that the frame is being forced.
