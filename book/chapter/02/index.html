Chapter Two: The First Echo

In which the grove answers a beat late, and will not swear to it.

An echo is a wish practicing. Practice is not a granting.

By the second morning, warmth had worked through sack and strap and settled against Boof's ribs. Behind her, the town maps still showed an ordinary field. Ahead, the berries' remembered route ran into birch and fern, where the air smelled damp—and somehow twice breathed. She stopped beneath the first tree. Nothing moved. Something arrived again.

She laid the egg on a mossy stone. Pollen hung in rings between the trunks, catching the light; her breath went out and came back thin, a little late. Then the hum returned. Again. Each time it carried something different: rain on leaves, the smell of supper, a door opening onto a morning she almost knew. The grove was trying out futures. None had happened yet. Boof listened until she could pick the returns apart. One came from the hollow cedar, low and round; another thinned among the birches; a third seemed to start behind her teeth. She closed her mouth. It came again. The egg lay still on its stone, and the moss beneath it had begun to dry. She looked from that small pale patch to the trembling gold in the air. So much happening overhead, and down here one egg warming one stone. She kept a paw beside it, where she could feel which of them was real.

Boof
1 min

The leaning berries had led her here—the grove beyond the last honest furrow, a place the town labeled as reserve growth and the workers called the listening thicket. Every step pressed a different unfinished fragment from the leaf-mold: half a lullaby somebody stopped singing, the first word of a prayer nobody dared finish, a clerk saying no such entry with the hollow confidence of someone who had never carried a wanting too small to say out loud.

Boof knew how towns worked. When a record-house is too clean, the wanting does not vanish; it drains downhill into the wild growth where no receipts are asked for. This thicket was where Boon.land kept every wish it preferred not to admit to having, every note it wanted to sound and didn't.

Boonberry
1 min

The berries here did not shine for harvest distribution. They were the wild cousins of the field rows—blueberries a wish had passed over long ago, each holding its drop of the granting—and in the thicket they pulsed like grains under stress: bright when an echo rehearsed a future they remembered the taste of, dim when the thicket put them back to sleep. Boof recognized several of her own past silences reflected in the dark skins of the fruit—small wantings she had swallowed instead of sounding. That was the grove's first cruelty: it kept what the town preferred not to receipt. Unspent wants. Unsounded notes. And no ant colony warmed this ground—the wild rows grew cold and stubborn, living off whatever heat the echoes left behind, which was why their light flickered instead of holding.

Echo
1 min

"Follow me," said an echo that used Boof's own pitch from years earlier—the careful, hopeful voice she had used back when she still asked for things instead of working for them. The path forked around an ancient hollow cedar and rejoined on the other side. Time here did not reverse; it rehearsed. In the suspended dust of the pollen rings there was always a second egg, a breath behind the first, shivering in gold spores—the wish trying out a future where it had already been granted, then setting the future back down, unconvinced, and trying the next one.

Boof paced the fork, nose to moss, while the grove offered rain, harvest, a road back to a better morning. Each future fitted nearly. That was the trouble. The wish stayed warm through all of them, and the hum kept coming back for another try. She could spend her whole life here listening to almost.

She took up the sling. In the pollen, the other egg lingered a breath behind, then stayed where it was. Boof walked on. Downhill, damp bark gave way to salt air; under the sand, something older had begun to answer. She listened without turning back.

The Fool · Goose That Learned Jazz

three wishes

1 min
Three wishes, says the old song. Wrong count, says the Fool. Three COMPLETED wishes — a wish isn't granted till it's finished.

He leaned out of a cedar where no door had been cut, pollen on mismatched sleeves, one hand cupped to his mouth like a brass bell. He'd been the honk in the treeline, of course. He sampled the trope the way you'd lift a plate off a stranger's table—"You know this one: a lamp, a rub, three wishes, a fool who wastes the third un-wishing the first"—and then he sounded it, HONK, one long bent note, and handed it back changed: the song never counts the finishing. Anybody can start a wish. Starting is cheap. The grove is full of starts. What costs, what really costs, is the last word, said out loud, with your whole weight on it. Every trope you sample, he said, tipping an invisible hat to the egg, must be returned changed, with interest.

Boof did not answer with a riddle, and she opened no book. She wrote the grove's first usable note on the air with her nose: an echo is a wish rehearsing; a rehearsal is not a granting; a granting needs a finisher. Then she trotted past him into the sand, because somewhere out there was whoever had left a wish one word short, and the trail smelled like the only honest question left.
