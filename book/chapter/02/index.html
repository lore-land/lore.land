By the second morning, warmth had worked through sack and strap and settled against Boof's ribs. Behind her, the town maps still showed an ordinary field. Ahead, the berries' remembered route ran into birch and fern and hazel-switch, where the air smelled damp—and somehow twice breathed. She stopped beneath the first tree. Nothing moved. Something arrived again.

Boof had been here before. Not here exactly: at the end of a berry, in the field, for less time than a sniff. Trees. The sack warm against her ribs. Nothing wrong. Now it was all round her, real enough to sneeze at, and she could not tell whether the berry had seen it coming or the grove was only saying it back. In a place that answers a beat late, it is hard to be sure which came first. She decided, as dogs do, that it did not much matter, since nothing was wrong.

She laid the egg on a mossy stone. Pollen hung in rings between the trunks, catching the light; her breath went out and came back thin, a little late. Then the hum returned. Again. Each time it carried something different: rain on leaves, the smell of supper, a door opening onto a morning she almost knew. The grove was trying out futures. None had happened yet.

Boof listened until she could pick the returns apart. One came from the hollow cedar, low and round; another thinned among the birches; a third seemed to start behind her teeth. She closed her mouth. It came again.

She tried the cedar first, nose leading. There was no second egg in the hollow. There was a wren, brown as a dropped glove and not much bigger than a walnut, sitting in a nest of shredded bark with its beak open, finishing the hum a beat after the egg had finished it. It looked at Boof as if she were the one who was late.

Echo-wrens, the woodcutters called them; every hollow cedar kept a pair, and they sang everything a beat behind, axes included. At the grove's edge a wren-call meant anything that came back to you. Boof sneezed. Three cedars sneezed after her, politely, one at a time.

The egg lay still on its stone, and the moss beneath it had begun to dry. She looked from that small pale patch to the trembling gold in the air. So much happening overhead, and down here one egg warming one stone. She kept a paw beside it, where she could feel which of them was real.

The leaning berries had led her here—the grove beyond the last honest furrow, a place the town labeled as reserve growth and the workers called the listening thicket. Every step pressed an unfinished fragment out of the leaf-mold. Half a lullaby somebody stopped singing. The first word of a prayer nobody dared finish. A clerk saying no such entry, with the hollow confidence of someone who had never carried a wanting too small to say out loud.

Boof knew how towns worked. When a record-house is too clean, the wanting does not vanish; it drains downhill into the wild growth, where nobody asks what you came for. This thicket was where Boon.land kept every wish it preferred not to admit to having, every note it wanted to sound and didn't.

The same workers said, usually over a second cup of birch-sap tea, that hob-deer grazed in here: deer no taller than a boot, cropping the moss on days nobody keeps. Asked which days those were, they could never say, and took that as proof. Boof found one patch of moss cropped short and tidy. She had been fooled by rabbits before. Keep the morning, not the week, the workers say of dogs; nobody had thought to ask her which day this was.

The berries here did not shine for the harvest. They were the wild cousins of the field rows: blueberries a wish had passed over long ago, each holding its drop of the granting. In the thicket they pulsed like grains under stress — bright when an echo rehearsed a future they remembered the taste of, dim when the thicket put them back to sleep. Their thorned runners hooked Boof's coat as she passed, like a shopkeeper with one more thing to show you.

Boof recognized several of her own past silences reflected in the dark skins of the fruit—small wantings she had swallowed instead of sounding. That was the grove's first cruelty: it kept what the town preferred not to admit to. Unspent wants. Unsounded notes.

And no torch-ants kept this ground. No lit streets ran along the roots; no small city held the winter off. Boof put her nose to a root out of habit and found it cold all the way down, like a house nobody has lived in for a winter. Torch-ants will not settle where a light comes back to them twice, the field-hands say; a torch that answers itself is a torch nobody can be particular about.

So the wild rows grew cold and stubborn, living off whatever heat the echoes left behind, which was why their light flickered instead of holding.

"Follow me," said an echo that used Boof's own pitch from years earlier—the careful, hopeful voice she had used back when she still asked for things instead of working for them. The path forked around an ancient hollow cedar, loud with wrens, and rejoined on the other side. Time here did not reverse; it rehearsed.

In the suspended dust of the pollen rings there was always a second egg, a breath behind the first, shivering in gold spores—the wish trying out a future where it had already been granted, then setting the future back down, unconvinced, and trying the next one.

Boof paced the fork, nose to moss, while the grove offered rain, harvest, a road back to a better morning. Each future fitted nearly. That was the trouble. The wish stayed warm through all of them, and the hum kept coming back for another try. She could spend her whole life here listening to almost.

Try it again, a little changed: that is all the grove is doing, the woodcutters say, and they will not hear it called lying. They say it at the saw-pit too, to a cut that binds — back the blade out, set it a hair lower, keep the cut that runs. Boof had let a whole day of tries go by. Only one had held. It lay on the stone where she had put it, and the moss under it had dried pale.

On her third pass, the moss beside her sank. Two prints, cloven and broad, pressed down a pace from her shoulder, then two more, walking away between the birches with nothing above them. The moss sighed up slowly behind each, like a cushion after a guest has gone.

Boof put her nose in the nearest print and got nothing: no musk, no breath, no warm hide, not even the green of a crushed stem. A track without a scent was a word without a mouth. The woodcutters call it the lag-stag, and say it is white, though nobody has ever seen it, which is a bold thing to say about a colour.

She followed the prints round the cedar and back. They came past her again, a beat late, and stopped beside the egg's stone, deep and square-set, as if something tall had stood there a moment, looking down. The moss rose. Nothing else did.

Toward evening the light under the trees went moth-soft, and the rehearsal moths came off the bark, dusk moths the colour of old paper, to sun themselves on the warm shell. Their wings were smudged with weather: drizzle on one, a hard white glare like light off salt on another, and on the biggest, a grey-green storm sitting on a hill.

The grove keeps its almanac on them. Woodcutters read the wings before felling, and are wrong about a third of the time, which is better than the almanac at the mill. Boof read only dust and lamp-oil, and the powder they left on the shell in the shape of tomorrow.

She took up the sling. The moths lifted off one by one, a little put out. In the pollen, the other egg lingered a breath behind, then stayed where it was. Boof walked on. Downhill, damp bark gave way to salt air; under the sand, something older had begun to answer. She listened without turning back.

three wishes

1 min
Three wishes, says the old song. Wrong count, says the Fool. Three COMPLETED wishes — a wish isn't granted till it's finished.

He leaned out of a cedar where no door had been cut, pollen on mismatched sleeves and cedar shavings in his collar, one hand cupped to his mouth like a brass bell. He'd been the honk in the treeline, of course.

He sampled the trope the way you’d lift a plate off a stranger’s table. “You know this one: a lamp, a rub, three wishes, and a fool who wastes the third un-wishing the first.” Then he sounded it — HONK, one long bent note, which an echo-wren overhead tried a beat late and abandoned halfway — and handed it back changed: the song never counts the finishing. Anybody can start a wish. Starting is cheap. The grove is full of starts.

What costs, what really costs, is the last word, said out loud, with your whole weight on it. Every trope you sample, he said, tipping an invisible hat to the egg, must be returned changed, with interest.

Boof did not answer with a riddle, and she opened no book. She wrote the grove's first usable note on the air with her nose: an echo is a wish rehearsing; a rehearsal is not a granting; a granting needs a finisher. Then she trotted past him into the sand, because somewhere out there was whoever had left a wish one word short, and the trail smelled like the only honest question left.

At the tablePlay in this chapter

The grove downhill of the field: birch, fern and hazel-switch, hollow cedars loud with wrens, and rings of gold pollen hanging between the trunks round a mossy stone. Everything said here comes back a beat late and a little changed. Past the inner ring lies the listening thicket, where every step presses an unfinished thing out of the leaf-mold — half a lullaby, the first word of a prayer — and wild boonberries hook your sleeve from thorned runners over roots cold all the way down. At the far edge stands a lightning-split dead tree, and beyond it, salt-glare.

Light Green noon strained through the canopy, brightest in the pollen rings where the air is rehearsing; it goes moth-soft toward evening, and the wild berries flicker brighter whenever an echo tries out a future they remember the taste of.

People

  • a sawyer at the saw-pit wants to fell the right cedar before the storm on the biggest moth's wing arrives — Reads the wings before felling and is cheerfully wrong a third of the time; will not hear the grove called a liar.
  • your own echo wants to be followed round the hollow cedar and back — One player's own voice from years ago: careful, hopeful, still asking for things.
  • a field-hand on her second cup of birch-sap tea wants for someone, anyone, to see a hob-deer and say so — Swears to it. Cannot say which days nobody keeps, and takes that as proof.

Hooks

  1. Prints on the felling line. Cloven prints have started pressing into the moss along the line the sawyers mean to fell, and nobody will cut while something tall is standing there unseen. Find out what it is waiting for — by the prints, by the moss, by what the wrens repeat — without ever seeing it. Nobody has.
  2. The end of the lullaby. A grandmother in the town has lost the end of the song she sang her children, and its first half presses out of the thicket's leaf-mold every seven paces. The thicket keeps only starts. The ending will have to be tried again, a little changed, until one holds; she will know it when she hears it.
  3. Warm the wild rows. A field-hand wants the thicket's berries to hold their light instead of flickering, but torch-ants will not settle where a light comes back to them twice. Find a hollow in the grove where nothing answers, or a way to hush one, and then persuade the most particular creatures in the land to move house.

Roll a d6

  1. Someone coughs, and three cedars cough after them, politely, one at a time — the third a little changed, as if it had a better idea.
  2. An echo in one character's younger voice says “Follow me.” The path forks round a hollow cedar and rejoins, and whoever followed arrives a beat after the rest of the party for the rest of the day.
  3. A pollen ring tightens round something one of you set down, and a second one shivers in the air a breath behind it: the grove trying out a future where it has already been given away, then setting it back down, unconvinced.
  4. Cloven prints press into the moss a pace from someone's shoulder and walk off between the birches with nothing above them. The moss sighs up behind each one. There is no scent at all.
  5. Toward dusk the rehearsal moths come off the bark to sun themselves on anything warm. Their wings carry drizzle, a hard white glare like light off salt, and on the biggest, a grey-green storm sitting on a hill.
  6. A patch of moss cropped short and tidy. Hob-deer, swears the field-hand. A very thorough slug, says everyone else.

Worth carrying out

  • Echo-wrens — A wren sings anything back a beat late: a password, a name, a tune you have half forgotten. The catch: what comes back is a little changed, and in the grove the changed one is the one people remember.
  • Rehearsal moths — Read the wings at dusk and you know tomorrow's weather, a little smudged. The catch: the woodcutters are wrong a third of the time reading them, and nobody can tell which third until tomorrow.
  • The lag-stag — Its prints press down a beat after it has passed; watch where they stop and you know where something tall stood looking down. The catch: no musk, no breath, nothing for a nose — and nobody, at any table, ever sees it.
