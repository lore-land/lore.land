In which two roads claim the same step.

The grove fell away behind her into a vast, wind-scoured plain of dry salt and baked earth, rime-white where the crust was thin. Boof stood at the edge of the Fool's Paradox. The crust forked and would not be two places at once. Under the rippled dunes, two older routes glowed like habits the land had never broken, both wanting the same next step—two directions of travel that had spent so many years almost meeting that they had worn a want of their own into the ground.

Then the ground spoke under her pads: one long, low note, and every salt flake on the crust stood up and lay down again like the hair along a dog's back. Along one route the glow brightened and moved, slow as a barge, toward the seam, and stopped a whisker short. Along the other, something answered. Boof put her nose to a crack and smelled brine, old and cold: a sea that had moved downstairs.

The salt-rakers at the edge of the flats called the crust the roof and the glowing routes the salt-whales' roads; on the roof, you did not stamp. Two whales, two roads, one turn, and neither in a hurry to give way. The egg against her ribs hummed louder whenever she faced the seam, the way a wish leans toward other unfinished things.

Boof filed it where she filed everything, by nose, beside the ant-streets under the berry rows. The field had streets too small for any map, and the flats had roads too big for one, and both smelled of somebody patient going home. She was beginning to suspect the whole land was like that: kept by things too small or too slow to be asked, each one keeping something else.

A leaning wooden signpost stood half-swallowed by drift-sand. Its painted lettering had flaked off seasons ago, but its long evening shadow stretched across the flats like an unyielding black needle.

Around its foot lay a ring of flat black signal stones, set out so long ago the reason had worn off with the paint. Signal-skinks basked on every one, finger-long and salt-pale, tails busy as a clerk's pen. They flicked — short, short, long — and each stone answered with a faint tick of warmth. Nobody could read it. When Boof's shadow crossed the ring, every tail stopped mid-sentence and pointed down the signpost's shadow.

At the tip of the shadow, right where the two routes wanted to become one turn, a curve of green glass broke the crust. Boof dug. The flats gave up a bottle, stoppered, sand-scoured, and warm in the way of a chair somebody has only just left.

The stopper turned. Steam rose, folded, found sleeves. Out came the Off-Season Genie, neat as a handkerchief and dressed like a clerk on his first day without a desk, smelling faintly of tea and brass polish. He looked from egg to dog to glowing sand.

“Two routes. One turn. Used to settle these before breakfast.” A wish, he explained, redirected what was already moving. These routes had been nearly meeting for a century. One coincidence and a Thursday would have done it. “Whales will wait for anything. It's the turn that won't.” He brushed his cuffs.

“Of course, I'm closed.” The egg was the last unfinished wish from the practice he'd shut down; he recognized it at once and looked away a moment too late.

Closed or not, he reached into the bottle's neck and drew out the bottle-desk. It came folded, the way a heron comes out of reeds: a leg, a hinge, another leg, a baize top no bigger than a tea-tray, pigeonholes, a dry inkwell. Its one drawer was labelled Thursdays. He opened it a finger's width, glanced in, and shut it before Boof's nose could count.

What escaped smelled of rain on shop shutters and a fish supper eaten standing up. It was the most interesting thing he had shown her yet.

The Fool trotted backward down the dune, spinning an empty brass lamp on one finger, salt crusting the hems of his patchwork. He had sampled the old song of genies, and he handed it back changed. It was never three wishes and a catch, he honked. It was a price list all along, and the grand wishes were the loss leaders. Footprints filled with sand the moment his heels lifted.

The Genie set the desk square on the sand and laid his instruments out on its baize: the wish-scale, its brass pans balanced against coincidences instead of weights, each one stamped small; the resolution glass, long as a forearm, ground to show any want larger than a loaf; and the want-needle, quick to find a want. He had made emperors with these. Moved rivers. Unmade two emperors at cost.

Boof sat still. The wish-scale stayed level. He set his lightest coincidence in the far pan, a met-on-the-stair no bigger than a lentil, and it sank with a clink, as if the question of her weighed nothing.

Through the resolution glass she was a smudge with a field behind her, a crop in the field, a harvest in the crop. The glass, which could not see anything smaller than a loaf, saw everything around her and nothing of her. The want-needle circled, slowed, and tried again.

“Below my resolution,” he said. “Emperors want loudly. Ants want small, but there are so many of them, all wanting the same warm inch, that it comes to a loud sound in the end. I did good business with ants. You appear to want—” He tapped the glass.

“Something these don't show.” He cleaned the lens on his sleeve, although there was no dust on it. Boof waited with the patience she used beside a cupboard someone had forgotten opening. The needle caught a glint of evening, pointed briefly toward the bottle, then swung away. The Genie moved the bottle behind him. Neither mentioned it.

Across the flats the wind brushed their footprints shallow, and the two old routes continued their long, unfinished turn. Far down the seam a whale groaned once, the way a sleeper complains about the blankets. For all his instruments, he had no measure for the length of this particular silence.

Boof huffed and looked at the seam, where two old routes still leaned toward each other like tired dancers. Could he not simply grant the turn, seal the wish, mend the map? "I'm not saying no," said the Genie.

"I'm saying reality is already moving very quickly in another direction, and every correction has to come out of somewhere. Perfect version costs a miracle. Mediocre version costs one coincidence and a Thursday. Your version—" and here his voice did something it had not done in years, which was lift "—I can't price your version. I can't even read it." A want too small for the instruments of empire. He had liquidated his whole practice without ever once seeing one.

He had one thing left that was free, because he had never found a way to sell it. He gave it to her the way he used to give it to emperors at the door, after the bill: “Build where the land already leans. Never cut a road the land does not want.” The emperors had all gone off and cut roads anyway. Two of them he had later unmade, at cost. Boof looked at the seam.

small want

1 min

Boof huffed and walked between the routes, paws in the seam, her weight enough to give their leaning somewhere to go. Under her pads the crust shivered; far down, two long notes found each other. Behind her the two lines settled into one turn. A long-held chord came home. No miracle was billed. The Genie watched the dust settle over work he would have charged a Thursday for.

On the signal stones every skink flicked at once — long, long, long — and every stone ticked warm, as if a message had waited a century to be sent. The Genie folded the bottle-desk back into its bottle, drawer last, without looking at the drawer.

"I'm coming with you," he said, tucking the bottle under one arm. "Not to grant anything. Understand that. I am closed." But a want too small to read had just done, for free, what he would have billed a Thursday for, and a small want was the one wonder his price list had never carried.

Ahead, where the desert gave way to saltbush and rising stone, Council Hill lifted its stormy silhouette against the evening sky, its storm grey-green, just as a moth had smudged it. The egg hummed against Boof's ribs, one word short of finished, and the three of them—dog, wish, and the only professional who had ever refused to guess it—walked toward the hill together.

At the tablePlay in this chapter

The salt flats past the last split tree: a wind-scoured plain of dry salt and baked earth, rime-white where the crust is thin, with two old gold roads glowing under it that fork and rejoin like a sentence being remembered. This is the roof of the salt-whales' road, and on the roof nobody stamps. In the middle, a signpost half-swallowed by drift-sand, its paint and its reason long gone, and round its foot a ring of flat black signal stones where salt-pale skinks bask and flick. At the far edge, saltbush, rising stone, and a hill under its own storm.

Light A harsh amber that breaks out of the grove's shade like a door opening, then a long level dusk in which every ridge throws a teal shadow and the roads under the grains glow brighter. All session the signpost's shadow swings across the flats like a black needle, and the skinks point down it.

People

  • a salt-raker wants to rake the morning's crop without once stamping on the roof — Walks flat-footed, talks low, and calls the whales the neighbours downstairs.
  • the Off-Season Genie wants to stay closed, and to be asked for advice instead of wishes — Neat as a handkerchief. Qualifies, counts and prices everything, then gives away the one thing he cannot sell.
  • a sign-painter from the hill wants to repaint the signpost's arrow, if anyone can tell her which way it pointed — Brush behind one ear; asks everyone the way, and paints nothing until two of them agree.

Hooks

  1. Tin needles. A peddler at the edge of the flats is selling want-needles of tin at a discount. Every one of them points down, customers keep stamping to make them work, and under the crust a whale has stopped mid-turn. Shut the stall without a scene, and get the whale moving again.
  2. Another seam. The salt-rakers know two more old roads, out past the signal stones, that have been almost meeting for longer than anyone has raked. No genie will take the job; the only one about is closed. Build where the land already leans: find the place where both roads want the same next step, and give their leaning somewhere to go.
  3. Where the sign pointed. The sign-painter will not paint an arrow she cannot vouch for. The skinks point down the signpost's shadow, the stones answer in a code nobody reads, and every raker remembers the arrow differently. Find out where it pointed before the paint flaked — or decide, and live with it.

Roll a d6

  1. The ground speaks under your boots, one long low note, and every salt flake on the crust stands up and lies down again like the hair along a dog's back.
  2. Skinks flick short, short, long, and each stone ticks warm. Let a shadow cross the ring and every tail stops mid-sentence and points down the signpost's shadow.
  3. A salt-raker lays her rake flat and lies down beside it with an ear to a crack. She asks you, very politely, to stand still until something has passed underneath.
  4. Far down the seam a whale groans like a moored boat complaining about the tide, and the crust under the party goes warm through the soles.
  5. The wind brushes your footprints shallow and brings the smell of rain that fell in another valley. Look back and the way you came is gone; only the glow under the grains says where the roads run.
  6. At the tip of a long shadow, a glint: an empty brass lamp half in the drift, a price ticket still tied to its spout in a hand nobody knows.

Worth carrying out

  • Salt-whales — Lie with an ear to a crack and the whales tell you where the roads run and which way something enormous is turning; walk the glow and you cross the flats safely. The catch: on the roof you do not stamp. A stamp stops the whale beneath you mid-turn, and a turn once stopped can take a century to start again.
  • Signal-skinks — Their tails point down the signpost's shadow toward wherever something is waiting to be found, and the stones answer. The catch: nobody can read the code, and the moment a shadow touches the ring the message stops mid-sentence. Someone always has to stand in the sun.
  • The want-needle — If the Genie lends it, ask it who in a crowd wants most and it swings to the loudest want. The catch: a small want sets it circling and trying again for ever, and it always points for a moment at its owner's own bottle before swinging away. He will move the bottle. Nobody mentions it.
