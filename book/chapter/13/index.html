ninth honk

3 min

The round room at the end of the river path was smaller and quieter than any map had promised. The seam-otters that had swum beside Boof down the bank turned back at its steps, where the rivers blended and left them no seam to swim. Over the steps, the lintel remembered the springs anyway. Someone had cut the boatmen’s name for the rivers into it: two springs, one bed.

It was here, on the polished slate at the center of the rotunda, that the finished wish did the one last thing a finished wish is required to do. It sounded.

The ninth honk — the one the Fool had carried unsounded through eight chambers of sampled tropes, the shard that had slept in the shell since before the frost — left the egg as one clean note and rolled out over the whole land at once. Over the dawnward field and its one kept furrow, over the grove with its newest echo, over Bonk City serving itself in slices, over Bone.land's honest joists, over two rivers sharing a bed without forgetting their springs, where the ferry-bell shivered on its post with no hand on the rope.

Boof stood in the middle of it with her ears full of gold and understood that she was hearing a return path made of sound: every trope the set had sampled, coming home changed, with interest. And when the note settled, the maps — the same maps that had beaded and refused since the first morning — finally took the ink. The first line they accepted, before any border or crown, was the warm furrow.

Nobody wrote it. The land set it down itself, line by line, having at last agreed to know what had happened to it. On the drying-line along the back wall, every map that had ever refused a line stopped dripping into the trough.

Boof went round the room by nose. Cedar, beeswax, iron-gall ink. Oak roof-beams cut with mortise marks, ^ where they lifted, . where they stood. Slate laid whole, without a crack, and so without hum-crickets. The quiet was its own.

The archive kept a little of everything the land had met. Rehearsal moths in a jar, their wings smudged with tomorrow's frost, then a blur where a day should be. Brace-boxes gone cold in a drawer; an archive keeps the recipe, not the warmth. On the high window, far from Bonk City, a pane-snail grazing a clean lane for the starlight. One drawer had no label but a pencilled line, a box for what you do not know, and it stood a little open.

Each ring of shelves ended in a gap, and in each gap sat a shelf-owl, grey as a page-edge, round as a loaf. When Boof put her nose into the nearest gap, the owl opened one lamp-oil eye and clacked its beak, the way a librarian clears her throat. The gap smelled of feathers and nothing else. The owls kept it clear for chronicles not yet written; they nested in what was missing, and would not have a nose in it.

Boof took her nose back. She knew a kept place when she smelled one.

Over each ring of shelves a rule was cut into the cedar, in letters a hand high. Over the maps: first ink stays — and the first ink on them now was the furrow. Over the mending bench, where torn pages waited to be heard out: listen before you mend. Over the songbooks, each with the same four beats pencilled under its tunes: the winch song underneath. Boof read them the way she read most things, by nose. The oldest cuts smelled of hands.

Beside the great book stood a bowl of cold spring water. Boof drank. The dust of the journey washed from her throat.

On the nearest shelf lay two copies of a map, held open by press-stones: three corners weighted, the fourth curled. One press-stone is always missing, even here.

In one, a white thread of path ran between a garden wall and the river. In the smaller copy, the lines touched.

Someone had penciled in the margin: leave room for the path.

Lower down, in the same pencil, a small unshaded fox trotted off the edge of the page. Under it: page-fox.

Boof sniffed the first page. Wet grass.

She sniffed the second.

Only paper.

Boof lifted her head and looked back the way she had come. Every ring of shelves broke where the aisle ran through it, door to desk, and each ring was packed so tight a whisker would not have gone between its books, right up to the owl and the gap at its end. Whoever built the place had left room for the path first, and fitted everything else round it.

The great book lay open on a cloth from the crosswind loom, whose weave still shifted color in the draught from the door. No one stood over it.

An oath. An apology. A genie's whole reserve, and one Thursday besides. The pages held what each had cost, each filed as a small realm in braces — {oath}, {apology}, {reserve & Thursday} — a room a reader could stand in for a minute. A brace that closed was a finished record. One that would not close was still happening.

Boof had carried a want until she knew its shape. Now the wish was spent and the note had sounded. On the desk sat the shell. Empty. Light.

She nosed its rim. Still warm.

happily ever after

1 min
Thirteen chambers, nine honks, and every borrowed trope gone home changed — warmer than it left.

The Boonberry Fool bowed, and for once said nothing clever. He set each borrowed thing back where it belonged: the found egg, warmer; the greedy wish, sweeter; the echo, somewhere you can visit; the small wish, a furrow that never closes; and the happily-ever-after, changed — an ever-after that leaves its last line blank.

He set his tokens on the shelves as he named them — a bell, a mask, a bone die, a berry — never in a gap. Last, on the bottom shelf, his shard-rack: eight dull shards, and the egg-shaped ninth slot empty. He had learned the set one shard at a time, and he did not hurry the slot that was left.

Boof looked from the book to the open shell-shaped door. The first map had refused her egg. This one held the road she had walked, all the way back to the warm furrow. She followed it with her nose. Grove, gate, river bend. Here was a place she had been afraid. Here, a place she had eaten.

It was not her map. Hers had no roads in it, only what kept what: the ants keeping the roots, the roots keeping the berries, the berries keeping their light for the pond, the beavers keeping the water slow, the kilns keeping every blow and forgiving it. Baneberries in the hedge-bottoms, saying things; boonberries under the ice, seeing kindly; boneberries feeding whoever came and promising nothing. The book had drawn where she went. She had drawn, without a pen, why any of it was still there. Where the egg went on it she still could not have said; only that everywhere it had been was a little warmer.

At the end, one line stayed blank. Every other page in the book had been glassed in the glassing press behind the desk, an upright thing of oak and iron with a screw as thick as a mill-shaft: pressed clear and kept, season over season, the older lines still legible under the new. A folio press does a page at a time; the glassing press did the land. It had pressed every page but this one. That was why it could still take a line. Its crossbeam bore the glaziers’ law: glass over, never scrape.

The line began with a brace: an open brace, and no close. A room with one wall unbuilt, which nobody could stand in yet and anybody might walk into. The Fool waited. The pen waited. Nothing came.

At last he set the empty shell beside the line, ready for the next wish. The great press ticked as it cooled. The shelves had room; the shelf-owls saw to that. The door stayed open.

Boof rested her chin beside the shell. Its warmth reached through the wood. Outside, a cart rattled past. A bell rang once.

She lifted an ear. Nearby, this time. It stayed nearby.

Morning spread across the farms. Smoke rose from kitchens; someone would soon be cutting bread. Far off, one furrow steamed in the frost. Boof turned toward the door. An early bee had got there first and hung humming in the shell-shaped arch, as if the morning were a bloom that owed it an opening.

Boof lingered with her nose just beyond the arch, where the cool air met the room's last warmth. The road smelled of damp gravel, horse, a little spilled grain.

Behind those came the less certain scents of places she had not visited. Nobody called her back to explain the ending. Inside, the Fool moved a chair away from the desk so there would be room to approach it.

Boof heard the legs scrape, then settle. She waited until the cart had passed before stepping into the road. Where its dust settled, a hat hung in the air at the height of a person's head — round-crowned, brimmed, touching nothing. It was a little farther down the road each time she looked. She could not smell who was under it. She could smell that someone was.

Behind her, the shell kept warm. The last line waited.

The Round Archive at the end of the river path, by night: rings of curved cedar shelves packed tight around one clear aisle, each ring ending in a gap where a grey shelf-owl sits and keeps it empty. At the centre a white oak desk holds the great book open on a cloth that changes colour in the draught; behind it the glassing press stands wound high, and along the back wall maps hang on the drying-line, no longer dripping. Rules are cut over the shelves, a jar of moths shows tomorrow’s frost, a drawer of brace-boxes has gone cold, and the shell-shaped door leaks gold. It smells of cedar, beeswax, iron-gall ink, cold spring water and owl-down.

Light One lane of starlight falls through the oculus onto the desk, kept clean by a pane-snail grazing the high window. Toward dawn the shell-shaped door outshines it, and morning comes in over a threshold worn smooth by journeys nobody has taken yet.

People

Hooks

  1. A record in the catalogue will not close its brace: something it lists is still happening out in the land. Go and find what is still going on, and let it finish by itself — a brace closed by force is a page that lies.
  2. Two copies of the same map lie on the shelf: in one a path runs between a garden wall and the river, in the other the lines touch. Someone means to build the wall. Walk the real place and learn which copy smells of wet grass.
  3. One press-stone is always missing, so one corner of every map curls over whatever matters. Track down the stone that went missing tonight, knowing that the moment you find it, another will be gone.

Worth carrying out

  • Press-stones — Weigh down a map’s corners and it lies flat and honest. One is always missing, so the fourth corner always curls over the place you most need to see.
  • The glassing press — The archive will glass a page for the party: kept season over season, the older lines legible beneath. Nothing glassed can be scraped — your mistakes are kept too, under the next layer.
  • Brace-boxes — The archive keeps the recipe, not the warmth: hold a cold box between your hands and it fogs into its old small weather for a minute. The hands that warmed it stay cold until morning.
