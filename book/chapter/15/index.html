Chapter Fifteen: Child, Parcel, or Door

In which one stranger is written down three ways, and all three are right.

Every record of him was correct. That was the trouble.

10 min read

Labyrinth
1 min

Bonk City has no straight streets. Its lanes curl like the inside of a shell. Its bronze posts lean together to talk. Every road into it bends at least twice on the way in, so that whichever gate you came by, you arrive facing the plaza and the pie; the city likes to see your face.

A straight line across Boon.land, then, does not come out at any gate the roads know. It comes out at the back wall, low down, at the parcel gate — the door the city takes its flour through, and its lamp-oil and letters, and anything else that comes in a box.

Boof reached it at dusk, two days of straight walking after the hedge, with the sky behind her gone the green-black of a bruise on a pear. A storm was coming over the far hills, turning things over to see what was underneath. Every pane of glass in the back lanes had begun to hum, the way Bonk glass does when weather is coming. It remembers being struck, and would rather not be again.

Pane-snails had clamped themselves flat to the humming glass. The lamplighters were out early, lamp-ladders folded into staffs, lighting the dent-lanterns one dent-bright corner at a time.

Ahead of her, under the hat, the figure stopped at the gate. Behind him, at the corner of the lane, one bronze post stood perfectly upright. It had leaned for two hundred years. It was not leaning now.

The Parcel Gate

  • a black bowler hat pressed level against the stone face of the lintel, resting on nothing
  • beneath it a line-drawn figure folded at the middle over something small on the stones

  • gutters along the wall ringing like small bells as the rain finds them

The porter was hauling the gate shut when they came. He was a broad man with a lantern on a hook at his belt and the face of someone who has been told by a great many storms exactly what they think of open gates.

The parcel gate was hung for three porters and has had one for thirty years, because of the storm-weight: an iron counterweight the size of a bread-oven, riding in a slot in the wall, that lets one man lift what three were meant to.

Outside the wall, on the cobbles, stood the evening's last deliveries: crates of lamp-glass, a barrel of oil, and eleven sacks of fine flour stamped for the Feast of the Uneven Slice. There was time to shut the gate or time to bring the goods in. There was not time for both.

He had chosen the gate. A Bonk City gate left open in a storm lets the wind into the lanes, and the wind in the lanes breaks glass, and the glass of Bonk City is made of every blow the city has ever taken. It does not like to take another. The bonk-kiln, which forgives old blows into new glass, is booked until spring.

The figure was crouched in the gateway — folded, rather, at the middle, the way a line folds — over something small on the stones.

The parcel gate is low. It was built for boxes. The figure fit under its lintel with room to spare. The hat did not. It hung at its usual distance above his head, and that distance put it exactly against the face of the stone.

“Hat off, son,” said the porter. “Gate's coming down.”

The figure looked up at the gap between his head and his hat — the gap already doing, as far as anyone could tell, precisely what the porter asked.

The porter looked at the gap too. He looked at the sky. The first big drops came down on the flour sacks with a sound like someone knocking politely on a row of doors. Along the wall the chime-gutters began to ring, roof by roof, telling the city which of its roofs leaked.

He had kept that gate thirty years by one rule: the gate comes down before the storm does. It was a good rule. He had seen what happened to the lanes the year somebody forgot it.

He held the gate up anyway. The storm-weight took most of it; he took the rest with both hands and his shoulder, and in a wind the rest is plenty. Up in the slot, the gate-pigeons who sleep on the weight every night rode it an inch up and an inch down and did not wake. He said nothing, and the rain came down on the feast flour, and the figure went on with whatever he was doing on the stones.

It took about as long as tying a boot. Then the figure straightened, all at once, with something in his hand, and stepped through. The hat did not come through the gate. It went over the wall instead, at its own height, without hurry, and came down on the far side at exactly the distance above his head where it belonged.

The porter let the gate fall. The storm hit the outside of it a moment later, like a latecomer finding the door shut.

Boof

button

1 min

Boof shook the rain out of her ears and went to see what he was holding.

It was a button: horn, a little scuffed, round, with two holes in it. Boof knew it. She had been under BoonWAP's table on the wet night somebody lost it, and had smelled the coat it came off, and had not thought of it once since. Lost things in Bonk City get kicked from lane to lane by the city's feet until they come to rest wherever nobody looks down. Nobody looks down at the parcel gate.

He held it up to look at. Round, with two holes. For a moment Boof could not have said which of them was looking at the other.

The porter, meanwhile, had gone out through the wicket into the rain and come back with the flour on his shoulders, a sack at a time, and stacked it by the lamp. Three sacks were spoiled through. The feast clerks keep a slip for this. DAMAGE, it says at the top, and under that, a line for the cause. Most porters write weather. It is nearly always true.

He wrote his own name.

Then he looked for a long moment at the figure, and at the hat, and at the dog, and said, “Right. I'll need to put you in the book.” Things that came through his gate went in the book. That was the whole of the world he was responsible for, and he had never once let it down.
Record

receipt

2 min

That night the city wrote him down three times.

The porter wrote him in the gate-book, in the columns the gate-book has. Received: one, at dusk, by hand. Shape: rectangular, with handle (separate). Condition: dry, which was more than could be said for the flour. Sender: none. Receipt: none. Under remarks, after some thought, he wrote parcel.

The carpenter came at midnight to look at the lintel, because the porter swore the hat had never touched it, and yet there was a mark on the stone: a curved line, faint as a fingernail's, exactly where something had very nearly touched. She ran her thumb along it and frowned.

Then, since he was standing still, she measured him. Carpenters measure anything that stands still long enough. Her door-rule has a hinge for every size of door; she unfolded it against him, and it stopped on its own with a click, the way it does at any honest doorway.

So tall. So wide. Square at every corner, with a line straight down the middle where a door would open. In her repair log she wrote: one door, standard, hung without frame. Opens — she held the pencil a while — inward.

In the morning the night school's mistress found him at the back of her room. Her pupils were practicing for the feast, as they did every winter, walking brass pie-dividers round paper pies to cut them into even slices for luck. Pie-dividers never come out even; the sliver left over is the luck.

After pies came combines. Each pupil drew two nouns from a hat, chose a sigil and a container, and chalked them on the lid of a brace-box warmed between mittens. If the nouns agreed, the box fogged into a small weather. It teaches a child what will hold together before anything is built. Chalk is cheaper than oak, the mistress tells them, and a box that will not fog has cost nobody a beam.

One girl's {frost & hearth} snowed warm, and the front bench caught the hearth-drift on their tongues. A boy's <river ~ moon> ran pale and would not stay. On the window the warm snow fogged the . face, the inside one; the # face, out to the lane, stayed clear.

Above the slate, in the mistress’s round hand, stood the week’s copybook lines, to be copied ten times each before the brace-boxes came out: write it before you argue it; one shard at a time; keep the morning, not the week. The front bench had copied the first line ten times and was already arguing about the second.

The figure was sitting on the end of the last bench with one line raised in the air, the way a pupil raises a hand, and nobody had called on him.

She called on him. He did not say anything. She wrote him on her roll — one pupil, new, quiet, hand up — and left the space for his name blank, because you do not fill in a child's name for them.

Three records. Each written by someone who had looked carefully and put down what they saw. Each one right.

Three Receipts

receipt

1 min

By breakfast the records office had all three, and the records office does not like a stranger to be three things. It sent for Mx. BoneWAP.

BoneWAP came up from the lower town clean-boiled and exact, crust laid like a set of good joists, the listening mallet under one arm, and read the gate-book, and the repair log, and the roll, in that order — slowly, the way a builder reads a beam, not for what it says but for what it will bear. They tapped each once with the mallet's felt head, and listened.

Then they pinned all three with brass rail-pins to the board in the hall where the city posts what has arrived: side by side, sources named, nothing smoothed into anything else.

“Which is the true one?” asked a clerk.

“Lean on any of them,” said BoneWAP, “and see if it holds.” They tapped the board. “A parcel is a thing someone is responsible for until it arrives. A door is a thing someone is responsible for keeping in good order. A child is both of those, for a great deal longer.” They stepped back. “I would not take any of them down. When the archive's glassing press has them, it can press all three, one over another.”

“Glass over, never scrape,” said the clerk; every child in Bonk City copies it at the night school.

The clerk, who had a form with one box on it, went away to find a form with three.

Before lunch the clerk had one, headed in a careful hand: [child, parcel, door]. A window of a form: three boxes to look into, none to stand in.

Under the three, the clerk ruled one more box, smaller, with no heading at all. “A good form needs a box for what you do not know,” the clerk said, blotting it. “Left honestly open. Otherwise the next thing through the gate has to be cut down to fit the last.” BoneWAP tapped the empty box once with the felt head and listened. It held.

Boof

feastchosen one

1 min

None of which was what the city was talking about.

By noon the word had gone round every curve of Bonk City, as word does there, bouncing off the glass: the crier's notice had been right. Someone had come in at the parcel gate in the storm, and they had come with no receipt.

By one o'clock the city had decided it was the dog.

It made a great deal of sense, once you heard it. The dog had come in at the parcel gate. The dog had no receipt; dogs never do. The dog had been under the table, long ago, the night the button was lost — several people now remembered this clearly, including some who had not been there. And had the dog not once carried a slice of BoonWAP out of the plaza and never eaten it? That is not the act of an ordinary dog. That is a dog waiting for something.

So they came for her, kindly, with a ribbon. They led her through the lanes to the plaza, where the pie was holding court, and where at the head of the long table the city had already set a second place, with a cloth, and a cup, and a plate, for the one who was coming. They sat her at it. The title of the one destined to spill exactly one pie settled on her the way a hat might, which is to say it did not quite touch.

Boof looked at the plate. She looked at the cup. She looked at BoonWAP, warm and enormous at the head of the table, who looked back at her with the patience of a pie that has seen a great many destinies seated at that place and served every one of them.

At the edge of the plaza, by the washtubs, the figure stood a quarter turn away from all of it, watching the woman who was washing the plates.

Bonk City’s back wall at storm dusk, where a straight line across the land comes out at the low parcel gate. There are no straight streets: lanes curl like the inside of a shell, bronze posts lean together to talk, and one stands bolt upright. The gate was hung for three porters and is kept by one, thanks to the storm-weight — an iron counterweight the size of a bread-oven riding in a slot in the wall, pigeons asleep on top — and its lintel is low, built for boxes. Inside lie the gate-book, the records hall’s board of brass pins and the night school’s paper pies; it smells of wet flour, lamp-oil, cold bronze, hot brass and pigeon-down, and every pane in the lane is humming.

Light Green-black storm dusk, a porter’s belt-lantern swinging, dent-lanterns lit one dent-bright corner at a time. By morning the light comes clean through glass that remembers being struck, and falls across three receipts pinned side by side.

People

  • the porter wants the gate down before the storm, and everything that comes through it written in the book — few words; signs his own name to his own choices
  • the carpenter wants to know how a lintel got a mark where nothing touched it — measures anything that stands still long enough, then frowns at the answer
  • the night school’s mistress wants every raised hand called on — round hand, warm room; never fills in a child’s name for them
  • the records clerk wants a form that fits whatever comes through the gate next — careful hand, blots everything twice, leaves one box open on purpose

Hooks

  1. Three sacks of feast flour were spoiled at the parcel gate, and the porter signed the damage in his own name. Find three sacks of fine flour in a city where every baker is baking for the feast, before the feast clerks come asking.
  2. Something small and precious went missing in the plaza — a ring, a key, a letter — and has been kicked from lane to lane by the city’s feet ever since. Find it the way lost things are found in Bonk City: where nobody looks down.
  3. A gate across the city was left open in the storm, and a whole lane of glass came down; the bonk-kiln is booked until spring. Keep that lane through the winter with pane-snails, chime-gutters and the goodwill of a glazier who owes nobody anything.

Roll a d6

  1. The panes in the lane begin to hum: weather inside the hour, and the glass would rather not be struck again.
  2. A late delivery stands outside the wall (roll: a barrel of oil, a cask of pear cider, a firkin of salt butter, a bale of lamp-wick, a hamper of candle-ends); there is time to bring it in or to shut the gate, not both.
  3. A lamplighter unfolds her staff into a lamp-ladder and asks someone to hold the foot while she lights a dent-lantern; each dent is a street the light remembers.
  4. The chime-gutters ring roof by roof, and one roof that has never leaked rings for the first time.
  5. A night-school pupil’s brace-box fogs open in the lane: snow that falls warm, or a pale passage that will not let anyone stay.
  6. By the washtubs at the edge of the plaza, a figure in a few plain lines stands under a hat that touches nothing, a quarter turn away from the crowd, watching the one person nobody else is watching. He does not come when called.

Worth carrying out

  • The storm-weight — It lets one person lift a gate meant for three. It takes most of the weight, never all — and in a wind, the rest is plenty. The pigeons asleep on top must not be woken.
  • The door-rule — Unfold it against anything that stands still and it stops with a click at any honest doorway: a fair test of whether a thing is a way through. It measures what it finds and never asks, and whatever it says goes in a log someone else will read.
  • Pie-dividers — Walk them round anything to share it evenly; they never come out even. The sliver left over is the luck, and the table must decide who gets it.

A bracework move

Does A window where the gate watches the storm: for a minute it shows, to the breath, how long until the weather arrives — enough to know whether there is time to bring the goods in and shut the gate as well.

Costs Watching changes both. The gate flinches at every cloud for a month, and the storm remembers the gate: next year it comes by the back wall first.
