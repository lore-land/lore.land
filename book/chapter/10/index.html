Chapter Ten: Bone.land Resonance

In which a hall tells the truth with its weight.

Skeleton first. A wish, like a roof, is only as grand as the spine that must hold it.

6 min read

Boof
2 min

Bone.land did not pretend to be theater. Its square-hewn oak beams rose into the morning like the ribcage of a sleeping leviathan, every mortise and peg left exposed to the weather, because this hall was the small constitution — the spine the land trusted to hold when the veil shakes.

Boof's nose sorted the place before her eyes did: oak cut that week, quarry chalk, tallow on the hoist ropes. Nothing painted, nothing glued. A peg-boy on a sawhorse was eating a joiner's loaf, rye baked square-cornered so it sits on a beam without rolling off.

She trotted across the sawdust floor, and the whole valley heard the frame answer her arrival with one deep BONK — not slapstick, but the sound of a structure declaring itself load-bearing. Sawdust came off the high beams in sheets; the joiners call that sawdust weather.

On a scrubbed trestle table between the anchor stones sat something new: a pale, plain pie, steaming faintly, ringed by plumb lines and tuning pegs. Its ingredients were pricked into the rim of its crust. Boof checked the list with her nose. It was complete.

"Mx. BoneWAP," it said, by way of everything. "Load-bearing. I listen to what things will carry. Set the wish on the frame, please."

Where BaneWAP was hoarded berries gone bitter in a banker's crust, and BoonWAP served himself and was never smaller, BoneWAP was clean-boiled honesty: no sugar it had not declared, no filling it could not show. In Bone.land, even a pie keeps its joints exposed.

Boof made for the nearest beam. "Not that one," said BoneWAP. "Read the mortise marks." Beside every joint a joiner had cut a caret ^ where the beam lifts, a dot . where it stands. That beam was a lifter, and you do not ask a lifting thing a question; it is busy.

Frames here are chalked on the floor in braces and marks before anyone raises them, because chalk is cheaper than oak and a mistake in braces falls on nobody. The marks are what is left of the argument. Boof found a dot on the sill beam and set the sling down there.

Boof's nose found fresh chalk before her eyes found the joiner, on her knees across the hall, drawing tomorrow's frame on the boards. The peg-boy asked, through loaf, why she didn't just cut it. She sat back, found two braces meeting wrong, scuffed them out with her heel and drew them again a hand's width over. “Chalk is cheaper than oak,” she said. Every joiner says it. The bench by the door is why: cut from the one beam in living memory raised before it was chalked. It is very comfortable. Nobody likes sitting on it.

The egg went onto the frame. A weight slid, a beam settled, and the little brass load-bells under the joists rang one after another down the brace until the weight reached the anchor stone and went quiet in the chalk. Boof could hear exactly where the egg was being carried.

BoneWAP lifted the listening mallet, felt-headed and worn pale at the grip, and tapped the sill beam once. Not a blow; a question. A beam struck hard tells you only that it has been struck.

A low note ran through the frame into Boof's paws. In the cracks of the timber the load-beetles listened. They click when a beam is asked to bear more than it agreed to, and every beam here is told its load out loud on raising day, the way a child is told its chores. Not one clicked.

The answer came up through the wood in gold: the smallest wishes have the longest arcs. Given the current, a small turn could travel generations.

Then BoneWAP tapped again and asked the other question — what if the whole river were forced round a throne? The load-beetles went off along the tie-beam like rain starting on a roof, ridge to sill. The corrections would come downstream, roof by roof. Boof shook the ringing from her ears. It stayed in the wood.

BoneWAP did not whisper the answer to a council or fold it into a drawer. It let the hall ring — and a hall that size, struck true, is heard in every kitchen of the valley. The chimney-swifts carried it farther than it meant to go, flue to flue, until kettle-lids hummed it on hearths that had never heard of Bone.land.

By noon the note had gone out on the pass road, to the tavern, to the Watering Eye, where people said it back to one another. Down in the well the remembrancers rose through the folded faces to listen, and the well-winch turned a quarter-turn by itself, as if the bucket wanted to hear too.

The Egg’s wish, spent small and true, would cost one lent coincidence and a spare Thursday, and its arc would outlast every roof in the valley. The grand wish a certain bitter pie intended would call in a generation of coincidences — a whole town’s luck, drawn without asking. Boof had heard it once before, for nothing, from a genie in the sand: build where the land already leans.

People stopped to hear it with baskets on their arms and tools still in their hands; a cooper forgot to set down his half-hooped barrel. Some hummed the note to check it; some asked a neighbor to say it again. It traveled out of the hall in voices that sounded nothing alike.

At the anchor stones two carters began to argue the sum, louder each time round. A joiner handed them a stub of chalk without a word. Write it before you argue it, they say on Council Hill; Bone.land adds only that you do it on the floor. They wrote it. Halfway across the boards they found they agreed, and were annoyed about it, and finished anyway.

A clerk from the hall of arches had carried the crack-gauge up the pass to compare floors. Set across the tie-beam, the brass calipers closed to a hair and hummed the note back higher and cleaner than the Rift's: the same law, a younger floor, nothing owed yet.

Boof heard one person stumble over the sum and another get impatient, then go back and help. By afternoon the saying had softened at its edges, the way told things do. A child kept the note going while an older neighbor finished the sentence. There was no bell to mark the moment the news became theirs.

The note was still in the beams when word came up the pass: the bitter pie had heard it. He had understood it. He had stopped waiting. Boof felt the tremor before she heard the messenger's feet. Above her, the pegs held; dust drifted from a beam; somewhere in the tie-beam a single load-beetle clicked at a weight nobody had set down yet. She checked the sling.

"Is this where the trail terminates?" Boof asked the king-post. The Fool answered with a soft knock on the honest oak — no. A true test does not end a story; it only takes away the story's excuses. Everyone in the valley now knew what the wish cost small and what it cost grand, and one bitter pie had decided the arithmetic applied to other people.

Nothing grew in Bone.land that had not been carried in. In her sling, beside the egg, the slice from Bonk City was still warm through its waxed paper. She had carried it over the pass without eating it. She did not know why, and she did not take it out.

She settled the egg deeper against her chest and turned downhill, toward the road the bitter smell was climbing. Behind her, the timber answered the shaking with small creaks, peg to socket, joint to joist. Ahead, the road looked much as it had that morning. She went carefully.

At the tablePlay in this chapter

Bone.land's great frame: a timber hall like the ribcage of a sleeping leviathan, every mortise and peg left bare to the weather, carets and dots cut beside the joints. The floor is sawdust over oak boards chalked with tomorrow's frames; the air is cut white oak, quarry chalk and tallow on the hoist ropes; small brass load-bells hang under the joists and ring wherever weight goes. Between two limestone anchor stones, on a scrubbed trestle, a plain pie with its ingredients pricked into its crust listens to what things will bear.

Light Clean white-gold morning through the trusses, sawdust coming off the high beams in backlit sheets — the joiners call it sawdust weather. By afternoon the light lies along the load paths in gold seams, and fresh black ink dries on whatever has been posted to the anchor stones.

People

  • Mx. BoneWAP wants to know what a thing will bear before anyone leans on it, and to say the answer where everyone can hear — Plain and polite, joints showing: “Set it on the frame, please.” Asks with a felt mallet, never with a blow.
  • the floor-joiner wants tomorrow's frame chalked right before the oak comes up the pass — On her knees, chalk behind one ear; scuffs a mistake out with her heel and says “Chalk is cheaper than oak.”
  • the peg-boy wants to raise something, anything, without chalking it first — Talks through a mouthful of square-cornered loaf; asks why not just cut it; gets told about the bench.
  • the clerk from the Rift wants to set the crack-gauge across one more floor and compare its note with the Rift's — Takes the note before touching anything; listens before mending; measures in notes, not inches.

Hooks

  1. A beam that was lied to. A barn-frame is chalked, cut and ready for raising day, and every beam must be told its load out loud. But somewhere in it a load-beetle clicks at a weight nobody admits setting down. Find what the plan is hiding before the frame goes up, or before somebody stands under it.
  2. The bench by the door. It was cut from the one beam in living memory raised before it was chalked. Someone wants it burned; someone wants it kept as a lesson; it is very comfortable either way. Find out what it was meant to hold, and why it failed, before the joiners decide with their heels.
  3. Ask a bridge. A footbridge downriver must carry the harvest carts next week, and nobody knows what it will bear. BoneWAP lends the listening mallet — felt-headed, for asking, never for striking. The party must ask the bridge properly and carry its note back up the pass, true, before the telling softens it into a rumour.

Roll a d6

  1. Someone crosses the floor and the whole frame answers with one deep BONK; sawdust comes off the high beams in sheets. Sawdust weather.
  2. A load-bell rings overhead, then the next, then the next: weight is travelling down a brace. Don't be standing where it is going.
  3. The peg-boy offers a corner of his loaf — spelt today, baked square so it doesn't roll off beams — for a good story about a building that fell down.
  4. Two joiners on their knees disagree about a brace, in chalk. One rubs out a caret; the other draws it back, smaller. They ask the party to judge. In chalk.
  5. The frame's note rings out across the valley, and anyone holding something forgets to set it down: a cooper his half-hooped barrel, a thatcher her bundle of reed, a player whatever is in their hands.
  6. Somewhere in the tie-beam, a single load-beetle clicks at a weight nobody has set down yet.

Worth carrying out

  • The listening mallet — Tap a beam, a bridge, a wall — anything built — with the felt head and it answers in a note what it will bear. The catch: struck hard, anything says only that it has been struck; and it tells you the load, never whether the load is worth carrying.
  • Load-beetles — A few in a timber will click when it is asked to bear more than it agreed to: a warning before anything cracks. The catch: they click at the agreement, not the weight, so a beam told a lie on raising day can keep its secret quietly right up until it can't.
  • Mortise marks: ^ and . — Read the ^ and the . beside a joint and you know, before you lean on it, which beams lift and which stand, and where a load may be set down. The catch: only on frames whose makers cut the marks honestly — and you do not ask a lifting thing a question. It is busy.

A bracework move

{.[chalk] ^[frame]}

Does The chalk stands and the frame lifts: a frame drawn on the floor rises exactly as drawn, no more and no less, and stands for as long as its chalk stays unscuffed.

Costs A full day's argument had out loud on the floor before anything rises, a week of the joiners' patience, and the floor itself — nobody may walk across that chalk again. One careless heel and the frame comes down as gently as it went up.
