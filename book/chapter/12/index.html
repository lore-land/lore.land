small want

2 min

The confluence smelled of two springs at once: one water chalk-pale and quarry-cold, the other berry-dark and sweet with far orchards. River tar on the planks. Under it all, warm shell.

Two great mountain rivers met beneath a wide, low-planked timber bridge, and it was there, muzzle over the rail with the egg warm between her paws, that Boof finally knew. Not a hunch. Not the shape of a want seen sideways.

She knew, the way a true scent is known at its end, and the wish was small enough to hold in a mouth: that the land keep one warm furrow open — always — for whatever arrives next without a receipt. Below her the waters ran in an unblended seam — berry-dark and chalk-pale sharing one bed without forgetting their springs — and she understood that her wish was the same shape as the river: a membrane, not a merger. Room made, nothing owned.

A seam-otter came down the channel exactly on the line, one paw in each water, rolling without ever choosing a side. Its fur dried berry-dark on one flank and chalk-pale on the other; boatmen swear there are two kinds of otter here. There is one kind, wet in both waters.

The Off-Season Genie had followed her since the third chamber, because her wants were too small for instruments built for emperors. He heard it and went very still — the way a craftsman goes still when the one job his tools were never sized for turns out to be the job he stayed retired for.

He laid his instruments on the rail one last time. The want-needle did not circle; it found Boof and held, like a dog on a point. The wish-scale would not settle at a coincidence, or a season of them, or a lifetime; it kept tipping, honey-slow, toward always. “Build where the land already leans,” he murmured: his oldest advice, and the only thing he had ever priced at nothing. The ants had kept that field warm every winter Boof could remember. She was only asking it to go on.

The Genie counted the price onto the rail. Once, he'd said a small wish cost a coincidence and a Thursday. This one asked for always. He emptied what remained in his reserve and kept his hand open beside it. “Everything left,” he said. “Plus one Thursday.” Boof looked from his hand to his face. Neither bargained.

Last he opened the bottle-desk's narrow drawer for Thursdays. Empty: he had spent them all on emperors. This one would come out of the week ahead. He folded the desk shut for good, with a small brass click like a shop door at closing, and left it on the rail.

Far upcountry, frost loosened along one furrow. The ground warmed and stayed warm. That night the torch-ants moved beneath it. With less heat to make, they could tend more roots; their torch-lines ran farther along the furrow than they had in years, and a beetle who had been cold since autumn — a load-beetle, a long way from any timber — found a lit street.

The berry rows widened through the winter. The ants had known the Genie longer than anyone, and they were the only ones who noticed what always had cost him. A stranger would receive a good coincidence. Somewhere in the coming week, a Thursday quietly went missing.

On the bridge, no thunder. Only water under wood, and the Genie putting his empty hand away. Downstream an ink-heron lifted out of the berry-dark shallows for the first time that season. It had stood so still that its reflection had dried into the bank, and it left the reflection behind on the mud.

By the week's end the echo-wrens in the hollow cedars had a new call, a beat late: a small dry cough, like a man clearing his throat before a price.

Then the last threads came down the road to the bridge, because a confluence gathers. Mr. BaneWAP arrived first, walking soft, his banker's crust gone flaky and generous where the awakened berries had re-sweetened him from the inside out. It came away in grey curls as he walked, thin as crown-wasp paper.

At the landing he stopped and rang the ferry-bell, which is rung by anyone who needs to cross the seam without wetting both springs, or who doubts the bridge will hold them. The ferryman pointed his pipe at the planks. BaneWAP stepped on. The load-bells beneath the deck rang under him once each, small and light, all the way out to where Boof stood. He had been heavier the last time anything weighed him.

He had no coin fit for the apology he owed, so he paid in the only currency a pie has: he split himself, clean down the crimp, and offered the slices to the road.

And Mr. BoonWAP — who serves himself in slices and is never smaller — received him warm, the way Bonk City receives everything, and showed him the trick of it: giving is not subtraction if the filling is true.

“I have always said so,” said BoonWAP, who had never once said so, to the ferryman, who had not asked. Mr. BaneWAP opened his mouth to announce something dreadful, from habit, and found he had nothing dreadful left. He announced instead that he would be very bitter about all of this, starting tomorrow, unless somebody was kind to him first. BoonWAP was, at once, at length, and without noticing that it had worked. It was the first thing he had ever foretold that came true the kind way. Boof smelled it go by: plum-dark at the bottom still, and sweet all the way up.

Mx. BoneWAP arrived last, clean-boiled and exact, with the oath-scale from the market stone under one arm. It weighed both slices and found them equal; the oath-scale ignores the hand that pays. BaneWAP drew breath to swear he would never be bitter again. “Promise the next stone, not the road,” said BoneWAP, who had it from the oath-clerk, and he swore to the slice instead.

Most days the rail-pins held the bridge's small news: ferry hours, a lost goat, a recipe for eel pie the pies present politely did not read. BoneWAP posted both receipts among them for any traveler to read: one slice given in apology, one slice received in welcome, sources named, nothing flattened.

Grey oath-moths came in off the market road and settled on the receipts, one to each; they settle only where a sworn word is true. And here was the quiet miracle of the small wish already at work — both receipts could hang side by side, because the wish had aligned them.

The whole confluence held: berry water and chalk water, bitter pie and boon pie, hall and grove, all sharing one bed along a living seam, a membrane that shares without giving the house away. It was the council's law from the hill, &[boon, bane]{table}, written this time in water instead of pine.

The boatmen have a plainer name for it. “Two springs, one bed,” said the ferryman, and went back down to his boat, which carries two bailing-cans, one stained berry-dark and one chalk-white. He has never once bailed one water with the other’s can. In forty years the boat has never once gone down.

The egg in Boof's sling was lighter now, and its heartbeat had changed from waiting to arriving.

Boof listened to the travelers crossing behind her, the different weights of their steps — a drover, two goats — and the pause where someone stopped to look down. Under the bridge, one river carried a darker thread for a while before the currents loosened it.

She followed the color as far as she could. Upstream lay the places each had come from, still there even after she could no longer tell the waters apart. The egg rested easily in its sling. For the first time in days, she shifted her shoulder without arranging it around a weight.

"Where does a spent wish go?" Boof asked the twilight, nose lifted toward the archive downstream. Nowhere, said the warm wind off the far furrow. It stays. It becomes a place you can stand.

Below the path the moon came up and laid a lane of light along the seam. At the landing the ferryman chalked it on his slate, as ferrymen do every clear dusk — <river ~ moon> — not to make the moonrun, which the river makes itself, but so no stranger mistakes it for a place to stop. You can go through. Nobody stays.

Boof followed the gravel path downstream. The egg rode lightly now, its song rising through sack and strap. Eight notes behind her, one gathering under her chin. Beyond the bend stood the archive. She could hear the river through its open door.

At the tablePlay in this chapter

A wide, low-planked timber bridge where two mountain rivers meet and share one bed without blending: berry-dark water sweet with far orchards on one side, chalk-pale water quarry-cold on the other, and between them a seam that shines like spun gold. Brass rail-pins along the rail hold the bridge’s small news; a cracked ferry-bell hangs at the landing beside a slate where the ferrymen chalk the moonrun at dusk; under the deck, small load-bells ring for every weight that crosses. It smells of river tar, wet hemp, crushed mint and pipe smoke, and a gravel path runs on downstream to an archive with its door open.

Light Late-afternoon gold skimming two colours of water. At dusk the moon lays a lane of light along the seam — a passage you can go through and nobody can stay in — and lamplight spills from the archive door across the lawn downstream.

People

  • the ferryman wants every stranger across dry on both sides, and nobody mistaking the moonrun for a place to stop — points with his pipe instead of talking; will not lend you the wrong bailing-can
  • an eel-catcher wants a wager settled: that there are two kinds of otter here, the berry kind and the chalk kind — certain, generous, wrong, and delighted to be proven so over supper
  • Mx. BoneWAP wants every receipt posted with its source named and nothing flattened into anything else — clean-boiled and exact; weighs before speaking, and speaks in receipts
  • a tinker with a handcart wants to get across without trusting the bridge, which means ringing the ferry-bell and admitting it — jangles when nervous; bargains with the planks

Hooks

  1. One receipt on the rail-pins has no oath-moth on it, and every other receipt does. Find which word in it is not true, and who pinned it there, before the next traveller relies on it.
  2. Two eel-catchers have bet their boats on whether there are one or two kinds of otter at the seam. Prove it without catching a single otter, and without either of them losing a boat.
  3. A traveller who has lost someone means to stand in the moonrun tonight and stay. Get them through the passage and out on the far side before the light leaves the water — without once telling them they cannot.

Roll a d6

  1. The load-bells ring under someone who swore they were carrying nothing; count the rings and you know how much they carry, if not what.
  2. A notice on the rail-pins (roll: a lost goat, a found oar, a strayed heifer, a fiddle left on the landing, a coracle found adrift) — and its owner is crossing the bridge right now, going the other way.
  3. A seam-otter rolls past exactly on the line, one paw in each water; whoever follows it along the seam stays dry on both sides for one crossing.
  4. Someone rings the ferry-bell (roll: a drover with two goats, a tinker with a handcart, a fishwife with a creel, a shepherd with one ewe, a carter with a slow horse) and needs to cross without wetting both springs.
  5. An ink-heron lifts out of the shallows and leaves its reflection dried on the mud; whatever else was reflected beside it stays on the bank too, until the next rain.
  6. A grey oath-moth comes in off the market road and settles on somebody’s sleeve the moment they say a true thing they had not meant to say aloud.

Worth carrying out

  • The ferry-bell — Ring it and the ferryman takes you over the seam without wetting both springs. Ring it only because you doubt the bridge, and the whole landing hears that you doubted it.
  • Rail-pins — Pin anything to the rail and every traveller will read it. Oath-moths settle only on true words, so everyone can also see which of your postings has no moth.
  • Moonrun: <river ~ moon> — At dusk the chalked <river ~ moon> is a passage of thinking water that carries you downstream faster than any boat. You can go through it; you cannot stay, and anything set down in it goes with the light.
