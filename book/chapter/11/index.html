Chapter Eleven: Paradox Bloom

In which a garden blooms before its seed arrives.

A grand wish grants easily. It is the finishing that never comes.

6 min read

Boof

egg

3 min

Below Bone.land the road ran through an old bloom garden nobody had kept for years. Quince had gone to riot over the walls, the blackthorn hedge stood white with flower, and a moss-thick sundial pointed at the wrong hour without minding. Boof smelled sap, sloe-blossom, a straw bee-skep warming in the sun. Then cold pastry.

It happened between one honest sniff and the next. Where the frame's note still hung in the air, Mr. BaneWAP stopped shadowing and simply took — banker's crust cracking with the effort, tin lockbox open and shut, the egg gone from Boof's sling before her growl could finish forming. He did not run far. He did not need to.

“Behold!” he announced to the bloom garden, which had not asked. “A wish the size of an empire, spent all at once, and not one clause about giving it back.” There was a clause. There is always a clause. He had written it himself, at the bottom, very small, and he never read the bottom of anything.

All around the rise, on the shut quince buds, the early bees were already waiting. They come a week before the blossom and hum at closed petals as if the bloom owed them. Boof had always thought it a poor way to spend a spring.

He had been saying it for a week, to anyone who would stand still: an empire, whole, at once. That is how a baneberry foretells, and he was made of them. Say a thing that will go wrong often enough, and loudly enough, and the world begins arranging itself to have heard you. Boof had smelled it on the wheel-tracks all the way down from the pass: not the crust, the saying, cold and plum-dark and sure of itself. Nobody had stepped on it in time.

He stood on a low rise with the warm shell in both crimped hands and wished the wish he had been drafting since the auction failed: an empire, whole, at once. And the wish — this is the part no one had priced — granted instantly. It was a moment of bloom before seed. Every bud in the garden opened at once, for him, with a sound like a drawer pulled out. The early bees went in and came straight out again. A flower opened on demand has not had time to fill.

Over the wall came the beekeeper whose skeps stood along its foot, her veil half on. Her bees were walking the open petals and coming away with nothing. She looked at the flowers, and at the pie on the rise, and said to nobody who would listen, “Open nothing before it has filled.” Then she went down the row stopping each skep door with a twist of grass, so her bees would stay home until there was something to fetch. It has been the beekeepers’ rule ever since.

Karmic momentum does not refuse a grand wish. It simply pays it out the way momentum pays everything: one coincidence at a time, minimum-viable, corrections deferred. A barn deed blew against his crust. A road bent to end at his feet. A distant clerk misfiled a fortune in his name. The empire arrived. It arrived mediocre.

It arrived in pieces. The throne came first, shed at the garden gate by a players' wagon that hit a rut at the convenient moment — a stage prop, gilt over pine. The throne smelled rented: hire-straw and other people's sitting. Banners came after, lifted off a dyer's line by an obliging gust, in colors nobody had ordered. Subjects arrived halfway through errands. One still held a loaf.

Boof watched the new emperor wait for the scene to feel complete. It kept arriving. It never did.

The crown came last, as crowns do. The wish did not make one; it found one. All spring the crown-wasps had been building in the blackthorn — grey paper chewed from fence rails and old auction notices, ribbed and turreted and very fine — and the nearest of their crowns came loose and settled on his crust. It was beautiful. It was hollow. It was still occupied.

Boof heard it before he did: a dry, papery hum, a rented thing with tenants. Every rented crown is wasp-work, if you hold it to your ear.

Bloom

berry memory

1 min

Deep beneath his crust, a berry woke. The wish had passed through him, leaving its drop of light just as it had in the fields. One seam shone, then another. Warmth worked through the bitter filling. He tasted what he had kept from everyone, himself included. Sweet. It had been sweet all along. Around him, the banners stirred. He made a small sound the throne did nothing to support.

“It was only ever rented,” he said, to nobody. Then, because a foiled pie always says one thing more than it means to: “The crown was wasps.”

Wasps know sweetness before anyone. The crown-wasps came down out of their paper crown in a slow grey trickle, walked the flaking crust where the sweet was pushing through, and drifted off toward the quince. The crown stayed on his head, empty now. It weighed exactly what it was.

The Wish That Would Not Finish

  • the egg rolls of its own weight, unhurried, certain
  • the crack-door on its crown is a coin wide now
  • BaneWAP kneels in his own empire, tasting his hoard for the first time
  • the wish remains unspent because it remains unfinished
  • rehearsal moths shaken out of the hedge into daylight, tomorrow's rain smudged on their wings
  • quince petals opened too soon, drifting under the rolling egg

Here is the paradox the timber had circled without naming: a wish only leaves its shell when it is finished, and a wish the size of an empire is never finishable. There is always one more coincidence owed, one more correction pending in some far county where a stranger's luck must be bent to balance a king's. The grant kept arriving and kept not being done.

The empire stood, hollow as a rented crown, and the egg in BaneWAP's hands stayed exactly as warm as it had been in the frozen furrow — spent and unspent at once, a note held but not released.

The banners had shaken the hedges, and rehearsal moths blundered out into daylight like guests at the wrong hour, a long way from the grove. Their wings carry tomorrow's weather, a little smudged. Today they carried the empire's: rain coming on over a court with no roof, arriving in instalments.

At the edge of the new court, a woman still holding her market basket asked whether she could go home. A banner brushed her shoulder. A rehearsal moth sat on the basket handle, opening and closing the rain.

No one answered; the emperor was staring at his own hands. Boof could smell bread cooling in the basket, a seed-loaf still warm on its underside, and dust rising where the throne had scraped the ground. The empire needed this woman to be a subject.

She needed to get the bread indoors before rain; she had read the moth. Boof watched her take one step backward, then another. The banner lifted, and she slipped beneath it.

His crimped hands softened. The egg slipped, tipped, rolled down the rise, past a banner dragging in the dust, through quince petals that had opened too soon. Boof stepped forward. It came to rest against her paws, warm as ever. The crack had widened enough to show gold within. She checked the shell with her nose: hot iron, sap, a little sweetened crust. Still humming. Still waiting. She left the crack alone. She had watched a whole garden opened early that morning.

That night, wherever she set the sling down, the ground beneath it was busy by morning: torch-lines bent toward the shell from every root within reach, and the frost drew back a hand's width, as if something under the loam had been given more to work with. Whatever was in the egg, it was not keeping its warmth to itself. An early bee was waiting on the sling's knot, as if the egg owed it something too. Boof let it wait.

The bloom garden below Bone.land, the morning a wish granted all at once. Quince has rioted over the old walls, the blackthorn hedge stands white with flower and grey with crown-wasp paper, and a moss-thick sundial points at the wrong hour without minding. Every bud is open and nothing in them is ready; on a low rise stands a throne of gilt over pine shed by a players’ wagon, ringed by banners in colours nobody ordered and subjects still halfway through their errands. It smells of sap, sloe-blossom and sun-warm straw skeps — then cold pastry, greasepaint and wasp paper.

Light Clean morning light that goes strange at the edges, as if the sky were being rented. By afternoon the moths’ smudged forecast comes true: rain over a court with no roof, arriving in instalments.

People

  • the beekeeper wants to keep her bees at home, doors stopped with grass, until the opened flowers have something in them — veil half on; gentle with bees, short with anyone who wants anything now
  • the woman with the market basket wants to get her bread indoors before the rain the moth on her basket promised — polite, apologetic, and one step farther backward every time you look
  • the players’ stage-keeper wants the throne back on the wagon before tonight’s performance two villages on — talks like a prompter, feeding you your next line before you have found it
  • Boof wants the egg back at her paws, by its own weight if it will come that way — nose first; growls once and means it; walks beside people, never sent

Hooks

  1. The players want their throne back by evening, but the grant keeps returning it to the garden gate one convenient rut at a time. Find the one coincidence that would let a throne go — somebody, somewhere, who honestly needs a chair.
  2. A clerk far off misfiled a fortune in the emperor’s name, and the deed it belonged to blew against his crust this morning. Carry it back to the household it was meant for before the correction reaches them the slow way, one bent coincidence at a time.
  3. The beekeeper asks the party to keep the garden shut for a week — no picking, no wishing, no shortcuts — while the flowers fill. Everyone who passes through has an excellent reason to open something now.

Worth carrying out

  • Crown-wasps — A paper crown from the blackthorn makes strangers defer to whoever wears it, for exactly as long as it stays occupied. Hold it to your ear and you can hear it is rented; the wasps move out the moment the wearer turns sweet, and then it weighs exactly what it is.
  • Early bees — Follow an early bee to whatever is about to open — a bud, a letter, a door, a decision. It waits there humming, and so must you: open the thing before it has filled and it holds nothing at all.
  • Rehearsal moths — Catch one gently and read tomorrow’s weather off its wings, a little smudged. The smudge always falls across the hour you most need to know.

A bracework move

{bud ~ honey}

Does A room you can stand in for a minute, where a shut bud dreams of being honey. Step in and you can smell whether the thing you are waiting on will be worth the wait — never what it will hold, only whether it will fill.

Costs The minute is borrowed from your own patience: the next time you wait for anything, it takes a minute longer. The box runs on borrowed warmth, and the ants under the walls will notice whose.
