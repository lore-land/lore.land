Mr. BoonWAP’s long trestle table took half the width of the plaza and smelled of hot crust, starched coarse linen, and warm cloves. Before Boof’s chin stood a brass place marker polished until it threw back a curved, solemn version of her own nose. Someone had tied the city’s chosen ribbon round her throat in a double bow.

The crowd along the benches clapped whenever she blinked. A baker in a clean apron leaned over to tell a glazier that only the destined one could look at an endless pie without drooling. Boof was not refraining out of destiny. She had already checked under the bench and smelled cold marrow near the cobbles, which was a far more practical supper for a working dog.

At the head of the linen, BoonWAP walked brass pie-dividers round his own rim and lifted out six generous wedges in a row. Each wedge steamed on a stoneware plate. BoonWAP remained precisely as wide as he had been at noon. “It is only arithmetic,” he told a delighted clerk, who was busy wrapping a spare wedge in waxed paper for her walk home.

Boof listened to the applause, and then she listened past it. Behind the heavy baize curtain at the far end of the trestle, crockery rattled in cold water without stopping. Every cheer at the front of the hall answered with a wet clink at the back.

Boof gave the city one full course of good manners. She kept her paws off the cloth. She let three elders shake her front foot and call her the keeper of the table. She did not growl when a musician struck a celebratory chord two inches from her left ear.

The ribbon sat round her neck the way a borrowed coat sits on a peg: warm enough, and belonging to the room rather than the wearer. When the seventh round of wedges went down the benches, Boof stepped quietly off her stool, ducked beneath the coarse linen, and trotted straight toward the smell of soap, wet brick, and cold spring water.

Nobody at the front noticed her leave. BoonWAP had begun a speech about generosity and geometry, and the brass place marker was already gleaming nicely enough to hold the second place on its own.

The scullery was half the height of the hall and twice as damp. Cold spring water ran from an iron spout into a deep slate tub, sending up ribbons of pale vapour whenever a hot stoneware dish went under. Along the brick wall stood stacks of clean crockery taller than the feast had been wide.

A woman with her sleeves rolled above her elbows stood at the tub. Her knuckles were red from the cold spring, and her apron was dark to the waist. Nobody in the plaza had spoken her name all afternoon; whenever a toast went up for the bounty of the table, another tray slid under the baize.

Beside her, standing ninety degrees to the tub, was the figure in plain lines. One circle, one rectangle, four lines, two dots — always ink, never blurred by the damp. The black bowler hung an inch above his head, untouched by the rafters. Each time she lifted a plate from the stack out of the rinse, he took it in both ink hands, wiped it briskly with a flour-sack towel, and set it down without a clink.

His right arm was a hair shorter than his left, from lighting an ant’s torch in the frozen furrow two days before. It turned out to be just the right reach for folding a towel corner cleanly round a saucer’s rim.

The washer-up glanced down at Boof, wiped a streak of suds off her forearm, and let out a breath that was neither complaint nor praise. “He doesn’t drop them,” she said, nodding toward the figure. “And he doesn’t give a speech about how glad I ought to be that the crust never runs out.”

If the pie is never smaller, the room asked without a single word spoken aloud, why do the shoulders behind the curtain still ache before dusk? Because endlessness belongs to the recipe, and cold water belongs to the hands. Keep the morning, not the week, Boof had heard in the night school copybook — and a scullery keeps the evening after everyone else goes home.

Boof lowered her head and shook the double bow loose over her ears. She picked the chosen ribbon up gently by one silk end, set it on a dry wooden peg beside the spare aprons, and sat down on the flagstone until the last tray stopped sliding under the baize.

At the tablePlay in this chapter

The plaza supper hall split by a heavy green baize curtain. On the streetward side stand coarse linen, brass place markers, and warm boonberry steam; on the brickward side run cold spring water, lye soap, damp towels, and stoneware stacks taller than a doorway.

Light Lantern gold at the long trestle; a single tallow lamp reflected in dark teal rinse water behind the curtain.

People

  • the washer-up wants dry towels, warm water for once, and a seat where nobody slides another tray past her elbow — dry humour, steady hands; wastes neither soap nor speeches
  • Mr. BoonWAP wants every guest fed and every place marker admired — generous, loud, genuinely astonished to learn plates do not clean themselves
  • a feast steward wants the chosen dog back on her stool before the second toast — flustered, clutching a brass seating chart

Hooks

  1. The scullery drain has backed up with berry pips mid-feast, and the plaza refuses to pause its toasts. Clear the cold-water trap before the stacks topple.
  2. Someone walked off with the brass place marker for the second place; three guests now claim the seat and none of them will touch a dishcloth.
  3. Find out who first hung the baize curtain between the trestle and the tub, and whether the old builders left a warm flue bricked behind the wall.

Roll a d6

  1. A tray of twelve sticky plates slides silently under the curtain before the previous twelve are dried.
  2. A brass place marker rolls across the flagstones and stops squarely under a stool nobody is sitting on.
  3. A baker opens the curtain to borrow a dry cloth, sees the plain-line figure folding towels, and closes it again without mentioning it to anyone.
  4. BoonWAP calls a cheerful toast to arithmetic; somewhere inside the sink, the cold spring water drops two degrees.
  5. A pair of pie-dividers turns up in the rinse tub, still refusing to mark an even sixth.
  6. The washer-up hands the party a warm wedge wrapped in waxed paper from the only plate she set aside for herself.

Worth carrying out

  • A plate from the stack — Set it at the bottom of any burden you share; it reminds everyone in the room who carried the last round before the next round starts.
  • Pie-dividers — Measure out the feast or the chores; they still leave an honest sliver that forces the table to speak aloud.
