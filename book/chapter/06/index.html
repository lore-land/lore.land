Chapter Six: Lanterns of Bonk City

In which glass shows a light it does not own.

A wish you can't say yet is still proofing.

6 min read

Boof

egg

2 min

Boof trotted into Bonk City the way you step into the acoustic chamber of a struck bell. The streets did not follow straight surveyor lines; they formed a Fool’s Labyrinth of curving bronze posts, rain-slick arches, and leaning panes.

The glass of Bonk City was not blown from river sand alone. It was made of old impacts. Anything struck — a dented kettle, a cart's bent rim, a bell cracked one frost-bitten morning — went to the bonk-kiln, which melted the blow out and poured it off clear. So the panes remembered being struck, and had forgiven it into transparency.

The kiln stood just inside the gate, breathing a smell like a hot coin. Its soot-furred keeper saw the gold crack in the egg's crown and lifted her tongs. “We take cracks,” she said kindly. Boof stepped wide. This crack was not hers to shut.

The lanterns were honest in the one way that mattered — they showed light without claiming to be it. Most were dent-lanterns, and every dent threw a bright crease on the cobbles: a street the light remembers. Some of those streets had since moved. Boof followed one round a bakery and came out where she began, smelling of bread.

Every one of them turned its faceted face toward the warm egg in her sling, casting its heat across the cobbles while owning not a single ember of the flame.

Then rain came, fine as flour, and the city rang. Its chime-gutters are tuned like a shelf of cups, and a sound roof rings bell-bright. Two lanes along, one rang flat. A roofer stopped mid-bite, said “Cobbler's, back corner,” and went off with the rest of his bun.

A lamplighter came down the lane, ringing each post alight with a small brass bell. At every post she shook her lamp-ladder out of its walking-staff shape in three clacks, climbed, rang, and folded it again. “Pie's that way,” she said. “Everything's that way, eventually.”

And ahead, down the widest winding street, the smell of boonberries baking rose over the rooftops like a second dusk — because the patron of Bonk City was holding court, and the patron of Bonk City is a pie.

“Every pane will offer you your own warmth,” said a voice behind the glass. Boof turned. Three reflections turned with her.

“Very flattering. Very little supper.”

In the corner of every pane a glazier had etched two marks: # toward the street, . toward the room. The # face travels, glaziers say; it meets rain and strangers. The . face stays home. This whole alley had been hung backward, and every pane was busy staying, showing Boof herself instead of the way.

But pane-snails had been at the soot, grazing it off by night in slow silver loops, and where one had passed the glass was forgiven clear right through: no Boof, no warmth, only a wet step, a cat on a sill, a corner. Boof sniffed a trail, chalk and cold rain, and went the way it showed her.

At the corner she lifted her nose. Through the rain and brass came the smell of crust. “There you are,” said the voice. “Glass can show a flame. It can't bake a pie.”

Every street eventually arrived at a round plaza. At its center, on an oak table, a warm boonberry pie held court. Rain had followed them into the plaza in the folds of coats and the soles of shoes. It steamed from the porters' shoulders while they ate.

A stall between the chestnut drum and the umbrella-mender sold brace-boxes: small glass boxes with sigil-etched lids, each fogged with a weather nobody had planned. The stall-keeper wrote every lid before she sealed it, so a box could be checked, and refused, before it was made.

{frost & hearth} snowed warm flakes on a thumbnail of hearthstone. (cat & bath) held nothing at all. Boof stopped at [grove @ dog], where cedars no taller than barley turned, very slightly, to look at her. Then every box on the shelf brightened toward the egg.

“Only what both nouns agree on,” said the stall-keeper, “and they run on borrowed warmth. A week, if nobody holds them.” Boof stepped back and took her warmth with her. The ants at home would not have approved. They knew where warmth came from.

Boof settled where she could watch the table legs and the lanes between them, the dropped crumb, the swinging heel, a spoon rescued before it reached the stones. Above her, conversation passed with the plates. Somebody had lost a button; somebody knew a shortcut home; a porter grumbled that the parcel gate's storm-weight groaned on the way up.

At the table's end two children walked brass pie-dividers round a paper pie. It never came out even. There was always a sliver left, thin as a paring, and they argued over it like judges.

Between courses a porter unrolled something on the table's edge for the smaller child to see: a page pressed flat under a sheet of Bonk glass, thin as a leaf and warm from his coat. Under the glass a drawn hill, a drawn dog, a smear where rain had once got in before the glassing and been kept there, forgiven.

He turned it over. A second page lay glassed beneath, older, the same hill drawn smaller. Folios, he told the child: whenever the story changed you took one to the folio press, which glassed it flat and warm over the last, so nothing known was lost, only layered. Boof put her nose to the edge. It smelled of pressure and heat and, faintly, of the day it was made.

The child licked a thumb to rub the rain-smear out. The porter caught it first. “Glass over, never scrape,” he said, the glaziers' first law, and set the thumb on the glass instead. It left a small print that smelled, to Boof, of boonberry. “Next time this goes under the press, so does that,” said the porter. “Then you're in it.” Across the table a glazier with lead under her nails nodded. “At the map-house they say first ink stays. Same law. Wetter town.”

The egg warmed her chest, the ovens warmed her face, and for a little while she could sit among people whose largest unanswered question was whether to have another slice.

The knife went in. A slice came out. The pie stayed whole.

Boof watched it happen eleven times. A lamplighter ate. A porter took seconds. A child who ought to have been asleep licked the plate. Boof checked beneath the table.

No second pie.

A server set down another plate and rubbed her wrist. “Enough for everyone,” she told Boof. “Always.” She looked at the washing-up.

Boof got off the stool. The server sat.

Steam rose from the crust. The pie considered the egg. “If you spent the wish tonight, what would you ask?”

Boof opened her mouth. A furrow. A door. The smell of next spring. She could feel the want, but couldn't get its pieces into words. She shut her mouth again.

“Good,” said the pie. “Still proofing.” At the far gate, something lattice-crusted shifted in the dark. “Take it out too soon,” he added, more quietly, “and see what you get.”

Boof paused at the last curb of the labyrinth and shook the rain from her ears. She carried a slice of BoonWAP wrapped in waxed paper, and the pie behind her was exactly as large as before, and her wish was still wordless and rising somewhere behind her ribs, and all of that was, apparently, correct.

She had not eaten the slice. It was warm through the paper, and she was saving it. She could not have said for what. The glass had shown her light without claiming it. The pie had fed a plaza without shrinking. There were ways of being warm in this world that did not require owning the heat.

Over the back wall, where no street went, a pair of gate-pigeons rose slowly into the lamplight, fast asleep, hung there a moment, and sank out of sight.

She stepped past the last glowing pane into the quiet square. Under the open stars waited an ancient stone slab with a deep circular groove carved into its granite top: the place where oaths are given in public and paid in the honest currency of fruit. The wheel-tracks in the dew ahead of her smelled of hoarded berries. BaneWAP had left the gates before her, and he had come this way.

At the tablePlay in this chapter

Bonk City at lamp-lighting: a labyrinth of crooked, rain-slick lanes where every pane was melted down from something struck and every lantern post is warm where it was once dented. Put a palm to a pane and you can feel, faintly, the blow it forgave; follow the bright crease a dent-lantern throws on the cobbles and you may walk a street that has since moved. The air is hot crust, kiln-iron, lamp oil and wet brass, and every lane winds, eventually, to a round plaza where a pie holds court and is never smaller.

Light Deep blue dusk, then lantern-gold pooling in every dent and crease; rain turns the cobbles to long gold mirrors and sets the chime-gutters ringing. In the small hours the lamps burn low, pane-snails come out to graze the soot, and the one clear pane in a backward-hung alley is the only honest window in it.

People

  • the kiln-keeper wants one more cracked thing to forgive before the fire banks; a kiln with nothing in it sulks — Soot-furred to the elbows and kind as a nurse; reaches for anything cracked with her tongs — “We take cracks” — and takes no for an answer, the first time.
  • the lamplighter wants to finish her round before the rain does, because every dark post is a lane somebody will walk wrong — Three clacks of the lamp-ladder, one ring of the bell, and directions that are true eventually: “Everything's that way.”
  • the brace-box keeper wants to sell a box nobody will regret, which means reading every lid aloud before she seals it — Writes first, seals second, refuses third; says “Only what both nouns agree on” the way a baker says “Mind, it's hot.”
  • the server wants to sit down for one whole slice; the pie is never smaller, and neither is the washing-up — Cheerful, rubbing her wrist — “Enough for everyone. Always.” — then a long look at the sink.

Hooks

  1. A street has moved. The bakery that smelled of hot rye is gone from its lane, though its bread-smell still comes through the wall, and the only map to where it went is the crease one dent-lantern throws on the cobbles. Walk the street the light remembers, find the shop, and get the morning loaves to the plaza before dawn puts that lantern out.
  2. Someone scraped a folio. A lane's page was rubbed clean to fix a mistake instead of being glassed over, and now three households remember three different streets and none of them can prove it. The glaziers will not press a new page over a hole. Find out what was scraped and why, and rebuild the lost layer from what neighbours, lanterns and pane-snail trails still remember.
  3. Something in the cobbler's lane has gone cold: the oven, the cat, the cobbler's temper. In a back room, a brace-box bought at the plaza stall — its lid smudged past reading — is fogging a small, glad weather on borrowed warmth. Trace what it is borrowing from, then decide: hold the box and keep its weather, or let it go cold in its week and give the lane its warmth back. The child who bought it has opinions.

Roll a d6

  1. Rain fine as kiln-ash, and the chime-gutters ring like a shelf of cups — except one roof, two lanes over, ringing flat. A roofer with half a bun asks whoever is nearest to hold his ladder.
  2. A backward-hung alley: every pane shows the party themselves, warmly lit, flattering and no help at all, until someone finds a pane-snail's clear silver trail and looks through it.
  3. The kiln-keeper spots something cracked in someone's kit — a cup, a fiddle, a shield — and offers, kindly, to forgive it into glass. It will come back clear, and it will never ring the same.
  4. By the chestnut drum, two children walking brass pie-dividers round a paper pie demand that an adult judge the leftover sliver. Whoever rules will be argued with, at length, by both.
  5. A lamplighter's lamp-ladder jams half out of its walking-staff shape. Until it is freed, three lanes stay dark, and the creases on their cobbles point to streets that are no longer there.
  6. Over the back wall, where no street goes, two gate-pigeons rise into the lamplight fast asleep and sink again; a porter mutters that the storm-weight groaned worse tonight.

Worth carrying out

  • The folio press — Glass a page flat and warm over the last one: the new story of a place is kept, and the old one stays readable underneath. The catch: a folio only ever gets heavier, nothing pressed comes back out, and the press refuses any page with a scraped hole in it.
  • Dent-lanterns — Follow a lantern's bright crease and walk a street the light remembers, including streets that have since moved. The catch: you arrive where the street used to go, which may now be someone's kitchen, and the crease only shows while the lamp is lit.
  • Brace-boxes — Etch two nouns, a sigil and braces on the lid and the box fogs into a small weather of only what both nouns agree on. The catch: it runs on borrowed warmth — a week, if nobody holds it — and something nearby goes that much colder. The torch-ants could tell you where from.
