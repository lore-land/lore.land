Chapter Six: Lanterns of Bonk City

In which glass shows a light it does not own.

A wish you can't say yet is still proofing.

4 min read

Boof

egg

1 min

Boof trotted into Bonk City the way you step into the acoustic chamber of a struck bell. The streets did not follow straight surveyor lines; they formed a Fool’s Labyrinth of curving bronze posts, rain-slick arches, and leaning panes. The glass of Bonk City was not quarried or blown from river sand alone: it was made of old impacts, every collision the city had ever survived melted down and clarified, so that the panes remembered being struck and had forgiven it into transparency. The lanterns were honest in the one way that mattered — they showed light without claiming to be it.

Every one of them turned its faceted face toward the warm egg in her sling, casting its heat across the cobbles while owning not a single ember of the flame.

And ahead, down the widest winding street, the smell of boonberries baking rose over the rooftops like a second dusk — because the patron of Bonk City was holding court, and the patron of Bonk City is a pie.

“Every pane will offer you your own warmth,” said a voice behind the glass. Boof turned. Three reflections turned with her.

“Very flattering. Very little supper.”

She lifted her nose. Through the rain and brass came the smell of crust. “There you are,” said the voice. “Glass can show a flame. It can't bake a pie.”

The Pie at the Table

Step into the scene

light lantern-gold pooling on the oak table; the egg and the pie the two warmest things in the city

scent boonberry filling, brown crust, rain on cobbles, warm shell

Every street eventually arrived at a round plaza. At its center, on an oak table, a warm boonberry pie held court. Rain had followed them into the plaza in the folds of coats and the soles of shoes. It steamed from the porters' shoulders while they ate.

Boof settled where she could watch the table legs and the lanes between them, the dropped crumb, the swinging heel, a spoon rescued before it reached the stones. Above her, conversation passed with the plates. Somebody had lost a button; somebody knew a shortcut home.

Between courses a porter unrolled something on the table's edge for the child to see: a page pressed flat under a sheet of Bonk glass, thin as a leaf and warm from his coat. Under the glass a drawn hill, a drawn dog, a smear where rain had once got in before the glassing and been kept there, forgiven. He turned it over. A second page lay glassed beneath the first, older, with the same hill drawn smaller. They called them folios, he told the child, and you glassed one whenever the story changed, over the last one, so nothing you had known was lost, only layered. Boof put her nose to the edge. It smelled of pressure and heat and, faintly, of the day it was made.

The egg warmed her chest, the ovens warmed her face, and for a little while she could sit among people whose largest unanswered question was whether to have another slice.

The knife went in. A slice came out. The pie stayed whole.

Boof watched it happen eleven times. A lamplighter ate. A porter took seconds. A child who ought to have been asleep licked the plate. Boof checked beneath the table.

No second pie.

A server set down another plate and rubbed her wrist. “Enough for everyone,” she told Boof. “Always.” She looked at the washing-up.

Boof got off the stool. The server sat.

Steam rose from the crust. The pie considered the egg. “If you spent the wish tonight, what would you ask?”

Boof opened her mouth. A furrow. A door. The smell of next spring. She could feel the want, but couldn't get its pieces into words. She shut her mouth again.

“Good,” said the pie. “Still proofing.” At the far gate, something lattice-crusted shifted in the dark. “Take it out too soon,” he added, more quietly, “and see what you get.”

Boof paused at the last curb of the labyrinth and shook the rain from her ears. She carried a slice of BoonWAP wrapped in waxed paper, and the pie behind her was exactly as large as before, and her wish was still wordless and rising somewhere behind her ribs, and all of that was, apparently, correct. She had not eaten the slice. It was warm through the paper, and she was saving it. She could not have said for what. The glass had shown her light without claiming it. The pie had fed a plaza without shrinking. There were ways of being warm in this world that did not require owning the heat.

She stepped past the last glowing pane into the quiet square. Under the open stars waited an ancient stone slab with a deep circular groove carved into its granite top: the place where oaths are given in public and paid in the honest currency of fruit. The wheel-tracks in the dew ahead of her smelled of hoarded berries. BaneWAP had left the gates before her, and he had come this way.
