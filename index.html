Read Chapter One Dawn in Boon.land · about 4 minutes New: Book Two Chapters Fourteen & Fifteen

On the coldest morning of the year, a working dog reads her field the way she reads it every morning, by nose: torch-ants keeping their hearths along the roots, boonberries asleep under the ice with a drop of light apiece, a pond that never freezes quite dark. One furrow is too warm. In it is an egg, and in the egg a wish nobody has spent, and no map will write it down. Somewhere behind it all a prophecy is keeping count: someone is destined to spill exactly 1pi(e), and a pie, like π, never comes out even.

Book One follows Boof and the egg through thirteen chapters, across a land where everything is keeping something else, until the wish is spent and the shell sits empty and still warm. Book Two begins on a Thursday the whole week forgot, with a hat that rests on nothing and someone under it, drawn in a few plain lines.

Back after a while, or starting at Book Two? The story so far (it tells how Book One ends)

Boof works for the land, not for pay, and trusts an honest scent. She knows her field by what keeps what: the torch-ants warm the roots under the snow, the boonberries keep a drop of light each, and the pond at the bottom never freezes quite dark. One winter dawn she digs a warm egg out of a frozen furrow: an unspent wish that no map will write down.

She carries it through a grove that answers a beat late, a council where a baneberry foretells trouble and the trouble comes because it was said, and a city whose patron is a pie who serves himself and is never smaller. A retired genie follows her, because her wants are too small for his instruments. Mr. BaneWAP, who is made of baneberries, announces an empire until he seizes the egg and gets one, as hollow as a rented crown.

Where two rivers meet, Boof finally knows her wish, and it is small: one warm furrow kept open, always, for whatever arrives next without a receipt. The Genie prices it honestly — everything he has left, plus one Thursday. The wish sounds over the whole land, the maps take ink at last, and the shell is set beside a blank last line, empty and still warm.

On the road outside, a hat hangs in the air at the height of a person's head. Boof cannot smell who is under it. She can smell that someone is.

At the folio house · how your copy is bound

Bound in what the land had to hand

Nobody in Boon.land keeps a book in one cover for long. At the folio house by the parcel gate, the binder keeps this one unbound on her bench, its pages squared and waiting, and six covers on pegs above it: rag from the mill, a pane of Bonk glass, a board of salt crust, a slab of oven clay, a schoolroom slate, a length of night-blue cloth.

The pages never change. Only what holds them.

She binds it in , sets the lamp , and leaves the shutters .

Rag, then. It was a miller’s shirt once; it has heard worse than anything in here.

Boof's map

The commons →

Boof keeps a map without a pen. It has no roads in it, only what keeps what: the ants warm the roots, the roots keep the berries in their light, the berries let it run downhill to the pond she drinks from. Three berries grow along it and foretell three ways: a boonberry kindly, a baneberry by saying so, a boneberry not at all. Her nose is at the middle, because that is where she reads it from. Tap anything she knows; whatever you have read past glows.

  • The ants warm the roots, so the berries keep their light.
  • The berries let their light run down to the pond, and the pond lights a dog's morning drink.
  • A boonberry foresees kindly. A baneberry says what will go wrong, and it does, because it was said. A boneberry promises nothing, and feeds you.
  • The beavers keep the water slow, so the well never runs dry.

Read it your way

  1. Tune the room. Daylight, dusk or lamplight; a nook with a window; text as large as you like; and whatever cover the binder puts on it. Pinch the prose on a phone and it re-sets itself.
  2. Choose a lens. Under any chapter's title, read it as Boon, Bane or Bone: the fairy tale, the absurd reality being left behind, or the notation underneath. Or tune somewhere in between.
  3. Keep your place. Your desk in the Scriptorium remembers where you stopped, finds a line you half remember, and keeps the whole book offline for a train.
